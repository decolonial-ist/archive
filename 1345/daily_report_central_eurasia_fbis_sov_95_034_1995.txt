Introduction

A year ago the president of the Russian Federation deliv-
ered to the Federal Assembly for the first time a message
on the Main Trends in the Domestic and Foreign Policy of
the Russian Federation. It articulated the strategic objec-
tives of Russia’s development and set the tasks for 1994.

What does Russia look like a year on? What has changed in
our life? What has it proved possible and not possible to
achieve? It is hard to give unequivocal answers to these
questions. The past year can with every justification be
described as a year of contrasts. It included the signing of
the Treaty on Social Accord and at the same time the social
tension in spring and late in the year, the conclusion of
treaties with the republics on the demarcation of areas of
jurisdiction and mutual delegation of powers, the armed
rebellion in Chechnya, the dramatic fall in inflation in the
summer, and October’s “Black Tuesday.”

By adopting a democratic constitution for the first time in
its history Russia was not beginning life “with a clean
sheet.” The entire burden of the past is still with us.

Russia is moving from one historical era to another at a

speed comparable to the lifetime of a single generation. The
fast pace of the transformations often leads to problems.

The old technology of power based on ideological and
political coercion is giving way with difficulty to modern
methods and approaches.

Rule by directive [direktivnoye upravleniye] rather than
the rule of law still prevails. The cadre renewal of the state
apparatus with competent people with modern ideas is
meeting with tremendous resistance. The authorities and
their representatives have still not learned to be open and
comprehensible. The result is that state institutions and
their representatives lag behind the flow of new problems
and tasks. This directly affects the quality with which the
mechanisms of power function and results in inaccurate
decisions.

Last year Russia continued to live in a rarefied legal area.

The number of laws which the country needs exceeds many
times over the number that have been adopted. The
legislative process is often overwhelmed by political
struggle.

New legal customs have not taken shape and legal culture
is poor. It is important to realize that respect for the law in
society will only take root once the authorities respect the
aw.

The judicial system has not yet become an equal, truly
independent and authoritative branch of power. The Con-
stitutional Court has been inactive for a year. The prin-
ciple of the direct application of the Constitution is virtu-
ally not being implemented.

A decade is coming to an end which has split the lives of the
present generation into two eras. While transforming the
political system, the economy, and public consciousness,
we have not hitherto been able to escape from the burden
of former prejudices, ideological dogmas, and standards of
political action.

The quest for models to imitate or attempts to form and
urgently implement our own ideal model for social devel-
opment are continuing.

The old myths cannot be overcome overnight. Most of
Russia’s citizens grew up and were raised in another world,
which was radically different from the one that we are
entering.

Our civil society is only in the formative stage and has still
not become an effective monitor [kontroler] of state insti-
tutions. Like the new authorities, it has suffered growing
pains.

We still do not perceive the state as one of the most
significant achievements of world history and social cul-
ture. Without it civilized human communal life is impos-
sible. A state does not only mean the institutions of power
and the officials employed therein. It is primarily a polit-
ical organization of all the country’s citizens, their joint
asset and common cause. That is why opposition to
statehood as such is extremely dangerous.

Early in the century the idea of freedom and justice was set
against the idea of statehood in Russia. And this confron-
tation was taken to such an extent that the existing state
system was overturned and a state based on political terror
arose from the ruins. The danger of this mistake is also
recurring today. The idea of statehood is being set in an
equally furious and unheeding way against the principles
of observance of human and civil rights and other demo-
cratic values.

Some political forces are not ceasing their attempts to
“bring down” the government and provoke the “dissolu-
tion” of the State Duma and are demanding over and over
early presidential and parliamentary elections. The tradi-
tional “quest for enemies,” social and political intolerance,
and the unscrupulous choice of methods are, like it or not,
undermining the very foundations of the country’s via-
bility, its security and integrity.

In these conditions responsibility for the fate of the country
rests equally with state institutions and society.

A constructive stance is one which expresses civic spirit,
that is, active conscious participation in the affairs of the

state, the use of all the rights and freedoms guaranteed by
the Constitution, and at the same time the voluntary
fulfillment of one’s constitutional obligations.

The experience of the last year has shown that the main
objectives in the deve!tpment of society and the state as
defined by the first Message were accepted by various
political forces. That is no coincidence since the central
theme of the Message—the strengthening of the Russian
state—was based on the fundamental legal, democratic,
and federative principles of the Constitution, on its main

It is now clear that one can define the general positions and

But no matter what goals society sets the authorities, their
implementation depends on the effectiveness of state insti-
tutions, on their ability to find timely and correct answers to
the most acute questions of social development within the

To this end the following are essential:

—the radical transformation of state practice and cadre
policy, and unswerving observance by the state’s repre-
sentatives of laws and ethical norms;

—a dramatic increase in the quality of lawmaking activity;

—the development of a practice for the administration of
laws which would ensure the direct application of the
Constitution, primarily in the sphere of the defense of
Russian citizens’ rights and freedoms.

The year 1994 has ended. It is hard and probably not
necessary to try to determine who, which branch or organ
of state power, made the greatest positive contribution or,
on the contrary, is more to blame than the cthers for the
fact that it has not proved possible to accomplish much of
what was planned.

Something else is far more important, namely to produce
an objective assessment of the experience of 1994, to find
a common understanding of the problems of 1995 and the
ways of resolving them, to muster the political will, and to
act in a coordinated nianner.

In accordance with the powers laid down in Point “f” of
Article 84 of the Russian Federation Constitution I
address my annual Message on the situation in the country
and the main trends in domestic and foreign policy to the
deputies of the Federal Assembly’s chambers and to all
Russia’s citizens.

1.1. The Economic Potential and Future of Russia

Russia will have not have any reliable guarantees of its
sovereignty, independence, and integrity before it over-
comes the economic crisis.

Financial stabilization, the growth of savings, their trans-
formation into investment, the revival of production and
positive changes in its structure, and improvements to the
quality of citizens’ lives—this is the road to economic
improvement and the way to reach the stage of a steady
upturn. A certain part of the way was traveled in 1994. It
was less than was indicated in last year’s Message.

The 1994 Message was the first strategic document stem-
ming from the new Constitution. The resolution of a
number of tasks within it—such large-scale tasks as
restructuring or the creation of a securities market, for
instance—will take more than one year. How close the
economy has reached the goals defined by the 1994 Mes-
sage—that should be the most important criterion for
assessing its fulfillment.

The past year has brought results which cannot be unam-
biguously assessed. In certain areas the progress was tan-
gible, in others it was unnoticeable—we were even unable
to avoid some backtracking.

But, on the whole, 1994 was not lost to economic reform.
Although the start of last year was marked by a sharp fall
in production, signs of stabilization then emerged. And not
only in extractive sectors, but in certain processing sectors
of industry as well.

Annual inflation was three times less than it was in 1993,
and almost seven times less than it was in 1992. The
relatively tight controls over the size of the federal budget
deficit, the abandonment of soft loans, and the introduc-
tion of real interest rates by the Central Bank had an effect.

As a result savings grew substantially and, consequently,
the financial prerequisites for improving investment activity

emerged.

Large-scale privatization was carried out within a tight
schedule. By the end of 1994 more than 110,000 state and
municipal enterprises had changed their form of owner-
ship. The nonstate sector, producing 62 percent of GDP,

now dominates the Russian economy.

Privatization has given a powerful boost to the develop-
ment of a securities market. In 1994 the volume of state
treasury bond: alone increased almost 90-fold to around
28 trillion ribles [R]. The potential of the market for
shares in privatized enterprises is great.

Market infiastructure (banks, trading, financial, and insur-
ance companies), which used to be very weak, made rapid
progress. Largely for this reason the share of services in
GDP has increased from 30 percent in 1989 to 51 percent
in 1994,

The economy has become more open. The motivation for
economic activity has changed substantially.

in 1994 there was also progress in the direction of other

goals set out in the Message:

—Part One of the Civil Code (the legal basis for the
market economy) was adopted;

—work was launched to identify and reorganize insol-
vent enterprises;

But it has to be honestly admitted that a great deal of what
was envisaged by the Message has not been carried out—
not only because of the long-term nature of the problems
facing us.

Considerable reserves for the creation of new legislation
remain unused. There is still no modern land, taxation,
housing, or labor legislation or laws on banks, joint-stock
companies, or the securities market.

A series of presidential edicts was adopted in order to close
the yawning gaps in the legislative fabric and to ensure a
normative base for the resolution of the most acute socio-
economic problems. But the economy should be regulated
not by edicts but by laws. Most edicts will lapse when the
corresponding laws come into force.

A lot of mistakes have been made by the government as
well. It has not proved possible to take advantage of the
relatively favorable economic situation that had emerged
by last summer, or to begin the mass transformation of
savings into investment. As a result, a number of industrial
sectors—particularly in the high-tech field—have found
themselves in a very difficult position.

Although the positive trend toward a fall in inflation
continued for a relatively long time, it did not persist all
year. A sharp fall in the ruble rate was allowed to occur,
and the rate of price growth had increased again by the
year’s end.

The selective structural policy stated by the government
did not go ahead owing to an acute shortage of budget
resources. The ideas of transforming the economy through
the selective funding of projects in “growth spots”
remained unimplemented.

There are still no modern markets in land and real estate.
It was only recently that we embarked on the regulation of
the securities market. To all intents and purposes we have

been unable to simplify the procedures regulating the
creation of new enterprises.

As a result, the market’s creative energy has to a consider-
able extent been unutilized.

Despite certain positive changes, an effective system for
running enterprises belonging to the state has not been
organized.

We are having to pay for the slow progress of agrarian
reform in the shape of a severe crisis in agriculture. The
elaboration and adoption of the Land Code have clearly
been delayed.

As before, the tax system is failing to stimulate investment
and production. The intolerable mass concealment of
income from taxation continues. Financial discipline is
poor, and this, together with inflation, has been one of the
main reasons for the continuing defaulting crisis.

Foreign trade regulations have been broken—primarily in
the export of energy sources and nonferrous metals—and

Among the main reasons for the failures of 1994 were:

—the lack of synchronization between market processes
and the state’s actions: In certain cases the authorities
lagged behind market dynamics, while in other cases,
when market transformations should have been tight-
ened up, they were unjustifiably sluggish;

—the low level of effectiveness of all branches of power,

—the serious error on the income side of the budget,
which forced the government to conduct economic
policy in the context of an acute shortage of financial

In 1995 it will be necessary to make progress in earnest
toward financial stability, to preserve and stabilize sav-
ings, to achieve an increase in investment activity, and to
turn the structural crisis into a controllable process. It will
be necessary to ensure predictability in the economic
situation, give enterprises a chance to plan their invest-
ment, and, in short, normalize the economic climate. Only
then will the accumulated financial capital start working
for the needs of production.

The task for 1995 will be to shape the initial conditions and

technological base needed for economic development.

We are faced in 1995 with problems equal in terms of their
complexity and scale to those we had to tackle at the start
of 1992. The serious task is to get through the transition to

mentally different tactics, is quite another.
While considerabie experience has been built up in the

market economy has to be formed virtually anew.
Financial stabilization and strengthening of the ruble are
the main precondition.

Consistent reduction of the level of inflation occurred in
the period lasting from the fall of 1993 to the end of
summer 1994. Prices have been going up again since
September 1994.

Taking a crucial step toward completing financial stabili-
zation, suppressing inflation, sustaining real rates of
interest, and strengthening the ruble constitute a very
important task for the government and the Central Bank in
1995.

Two serious attempts to halt inflation were made during
the years of reform—in the first half of 1992 and at the end
of 1993 and start of 1994. For various reasons they were
not seen through. The third attempt must be successful.

Substantially reducing inflation and rendering it predictable
is the target guideline for 1995. If we meet this condition
we will be able to start realistically creating the potential
for economic growth.

Only then will production investment become effective;
will enterprises with a long production cycle start to
operate steadily, and it is they which largely determine
Russia’s scientific and technical propects; will enterprises’
working capital stabilize, and consequently a very difffi-
cult problem which har been sapping Russian industry
throughout the reform years be resolved; will favorable
conditions for retooling home industry emerge; and will
real recovery of the financial market, which will be shifted
away from short-term speculative operations toward mobi-
lizing savings for long-term investment projects, occur.
Beating inflation and achieving financial stabilization is a
task within our powers. It is also clear that checking
inflation at the cost of deferring budget payments yields a
temporary and, on the whole, illusory result.

Only very rapid adoption by the Federal Assembly of a
realistic budget, covering the budget deficit by noninfla-
tionary means, securing the maximum receipt of resources
on the revenue side, and ensuring coordinated action
between the government and the Central Bank will yield
real success. Tough control over the targeted use cf budget
resources is necessary. The main role here belongs to the
federal exchequer.

Unlike last year, the state must in 1995 meet its financial
commitments without fail. At the same time no institution
of state power—be it the Federal Assembly, the govern-
ment, or the president—is entitled to make decisions
which undermine the budget and intensify inflation. If
discipline is tightened up, it must be at every level of
power, if savings are made, they must be made by all
without exception.

It will then be possible to count on reducing and stabilizing
inflationary expectations. It is time to get used to it: There
is no more “easy” money to be blown into our pocket or
accounts by the crazy winds cf inflation; there is and will
only be “hard-won” money, money earned by labor.

It is not enough, however, to achieve financial stabiliza-
tion. It is also necessary to know how to employ the fruits
of it with the ultimate aim of not just stabilizing produc-
tion but improving its efficiency. Efficient prodiction is a
most important element of economic growth potential.

The Federal Assembly must expeditiously adopt amend-
ments to the law on foreign investments.

The approach to foreign investments will be determined by
how far they assist in forming the potential for growth of
the Russian economy. This is where the national
interest lies. It is this which should be the guiding light
when elaborating the “rules of the game” for foreign
investors, including those participating in privatization.
We need only serious, reliable partners working for the
future and not damaging the national interests of the
country.

This year a Tax Code must be elaborated and adopted. Its
task is to stimulate investments and production while
safeguarding in full measure the revenue section of budgets
of all levels.

There are plans for the gradual abolition of individual
concessions on payments to the federal budget. Indulgence
should be granted only according to the law, in a uniform
procedure, and openly, so it can be clear who is benefiting,
on what grounds, for how long, and on what terms.

While helping the stabilization of production, investment
activity by no means guarantees long-term positive
changes in its structure. And without these, the potential
for economic growth will not be created. There is still some
time before high-tech sectors become attractive for private
investors. It would be hasty to place our hopes here on
market forces alone. State support is required.

It is time, in 1995, to begin a structural maneuver.
Its ultimate aim is to bring about a relative reduction of the
raw material sphere of the economy by means of an
increase within the structure of GNP of manufacturing
industry’s share, especially its science-intensive part, the
services sphere, and intellectual activity. Then it will be
possible not only to move onto the stage of economic
growth, but also to accord it a modern quality.

Long-term federal programs will help the matter. Not
programs like the ones we have now, which are spread
across the whole economy, but ones which are closely
forged around the main task—forming the potential for
economic growth. There must be far fewer programs, and
their quality must be far higher.

In 1995 the government and Federal Assembly must begin
to advance in three main directions.

1. Technological modernization of the Russian economy.
The aim is to replace the major losses of fixed capital
incurred during the period of the current crisis, and to
create the preconditions for stable growth of the Russian
economy at the end of the 1990's.

2. The strengthening of the national industrial infrastruc-
ture. The accumulation of resources, including budget
resources, with the subsequent channeling of these into
financing federal roads, ports, airfields, transport, and
telecommunications systems, will endow economic
growth with the necessary sustainability and help resolve
the problem of unemployment.

In 1995 we will be preparing to set in motion a mechanism
of state orders which has proved itself in many developed
countries. With time it will become an active lever of
structural policy. The state orders being formulated by
federal departments, regional administrations, and organs
of local self-government will make it possible to actively
influence investment demand. Acting within the frame-

An important mechanism of structural policy is the stim-
ulation of intersector capital cross-flows. This presupposes
the creation of a reliable legal base and organizational
foundations of a market trading in securities from nonstate
enterprises, banks, and financial companies, especially the
secondary sector of this market.

The structural turning point envisioned is impossible
without state support for industry at federal level and in the
regions.

It has to be admitted that organs of power in the center and
in the localities are tackling this task unsatisfactorily. A
relatively small amount of state finance is being distrib-
uted among a large number of enterprises, which is leading
to general underfinancing.

The continuation of state support for inefficient produc-
tion units will kill the economy. Federal and regional
budgets will not be able to withstand the heavy loads. In
1995 resolute measures will be taken allowing us to put an
end to this situation.

The entire system of state support is in need of fundamental
changes. Its strategic points of reference must be promising
projects and programs securing the mobilization of real,
effective demand and vital science-intensive and high-tech
production units and collectives working in critically
important areas.

Strict control to ensure use as intended, and the account-
ability of the recipient for the obligations undertaken,
comprise the indispensable terms for offering state support
in any shape or form.

Russian scientific and technical potential must become a
natural target for state support.

Our scientists obtain first-class results, and they are behind
the creation of many samples of new products which are
competitive on the world market but which, as in previous
years, are frequently left unclaimed by our industry.

The government and the Federal Assembly have to elabo-
rate a normative base stimulating innovative activity and
bringing investment into the sphere of science and tech-
nology. With the help of budget finance, it is essential to
support research guaranteeing the creation of new techno-

Targeted action will be taken to preserve science-intensive

units. Above all, this means a fully financed
defense order, the implementation of decisivns on setting

output at just a few enterprises. It is no less important to
learn how to use budget resources sensibly, sparing the

Leasing and other mechanisms of support for high-tech
industry will also prove useful. Other development
resources include well-planned privatization with the sub-
sequent involvement of funds from the stock market and
exports backed up by state support.

The integration of financial and industrial capital will help
to preserve science-intensive industry. This is a natural
process, and slowing down the creation of financial-
industrial groups is not only harmful but nonsensical. The
problem of financial-industrial groups, however, is not one
of the most straightforward.

In 1995 we will construct state policy in such a way so as
not to create a barrier on the path of forming effective
financial-industrial interrelationships. At the same time
we cannot allow financia!-industrial groups to be turned
into a factor for monopolization of the economy, a means
of gaining concessions and “cheap” state money for inef-
ficient projects.

The agricultural sector cannot get by without state support
either. In a country like Russia, this support will be there
for a long time yet.

Direct state support will be geared to the infrastructure of
the agrarian sector and programs defining the potential for
its development (selection, gene pool, high-yield crops, and
so On).

In the transition period the agrarian sector needs sensible
defense not only from foreign competitors, but also from
homegrown industrial monopolists.

1995 must become the year of real agrarian reform. Your
own farmstead on your own land—this is its long-term

Modern land legislation is urgently needed. The legaliza-
tion of the land market and its effective state regulation are
only possible on its basis. Only those who use the land as
intended are entitled to become proprietors, to own land.

A mixed agrarian economy, in which there is a place for
private farms, renovated kolhozes and sovkhozes, and
personal plots, matches the realities of Russia.

Other tasks for 1995 include:

—the formation of a sys‘em of land banks and other
elements of a market infrastructure;

—overcoming local monopolism in the sphere of pro-
cessing and agricultural services;

—the creation of an effective mortgaging mechanism for
land;

—the “scrapping” of chronically loss-making farm-
steads, followed by recapitalization and a change in
form of ownership.

The social sphere of the countryside has ended up in a
difficult position. Out of 150,000 Russian villages, only
around 10,000 are properly catered for. It is planned not
only to create favorable economic conditions to increase
the incomes of agricultural commodity producers, but also
to guarantee social protection for the rural population
when forms of ownership change.

Positive structural advances in Russia’s economy are
unlikely to become a reality without success in attaining a
drastic strengthening of financial discipline. Mutual non-
payments continue. A reluctance or inability on the part of
the managers of a number of enterprises to work in the
tough conditions of a market economy, and the lack of
accountability for the financial outcome, were factors
inciting the rise in mutual nonpayments. Nonpayments—
this is a means of survival, advantageous primarily to
uncompetitive enterprises, a means of exerting pressure on
the state with the aim of forcing it to give such enterprises
support. In a number of cases, nonpayments have become
a mechanism serving the mercenary interests of economic
managers.

The Government Commission for the Problem of Nonpay-
ments is as yet functioning unsatisfactorily.

The act of surmounting the nonpayments crisis entails the
implementation of structural overhaul of individual enter-
prises and the entire economy and the implementation of
the bankruptcy procedure for inefficient and insolvent
enterprises.

In 1995 the course toward integration of the Russian
economy into the world economy will be continued. There is
no future in fencing ourselves off from it, as this contra-
dicts the interests of the country and seriously complicates
the formation of the potential for economic growth.

As in other countries fully participating in world trade, a
proportion of the domestic Russian market will be filled
with foreign commodities. There can be no other way.
That a process of sharing the market between national and
foreign producers must be implemented is another matter.
What we need is a long-term state strategy for regulating

the domestic market and active talks with Russia’s leading
trade partners on the mutual opening up of markets.

Today the main thing is to guarantee the requisite protec-
tion of the domestic market with the help of tariff and
nontariff, mainly economic, methods. This tactical
maneuver does not contradict the long-term policy of
gradually reducing the rate of import tariff duties in step
with the growth of the competitiveness of domestic pro-

Those sectors of the economy which are in a state of
chronic crisis and which will take a long time to emerge
from it are in need of protection above all. This applies to
the agricultural sector, for instance,. It is wrong to close our
eyes either to subsidization by the governments of certain
countries of exports to Russia, or discrimination against
our produce on foreign markets. We will give the proper
response to actions impinging upon the interests of our
country.

The protectionist regime being introduced in certain cases
must be temporary and encourage not a preservation of the
past but rather an increase in the competitiveness of
national output. Russian producers unable as yet to with-
stand competition with importers will have to assimilate
new techniques with determination: The state will give
them time for technological modernization, but it will not
protect them forever to the detriment of consumers.

Flexible regulation of the currency rate and its synchroni-
zation with the dynamics of inflation and the money
supply will become a powerful reserve for the defense of
the domestic market.

To this day the problem of exporting capital from Russia
remains unregulated. Stringent customs, bankiag, and cur-
rency control has yet to be established. However, it would
de a simplification to reduce the matter to administrative
measures alone. The problem is far wider.

Today it is essential to do everything possible in order to
atiract capital to Russia and to get the investment process
organized.

It is necessary to think of the future. Russian financial-
industrial capital stands on the threshold of qualitative
changes in economic behavior. Already now, its resources
are so great that they need new spheres of application—not
only in Russia, but also abroad. This is a natural process,

1.2. Federalism and Local Self-Government

Russian history has decreed that the state’s sovereignty
extends over a vast territory and covers numerous peoples.

By virtue of the size of the territory and the huge diversity
of living conditions—historical, national, economic, natu-
ral—Russia has two methods of preserving itself as a
unified state.

One is unitarism, rigorously centralized government. It was
on this basis that Russia existed until recently. This
method is based mainly on the edict [komandnyy] prin-
ciple. The effectiveness of the authority depends here on
the might of nor-law-based [nepravovyy], coercive means.

Our society has adopted a different path of preserving the
state’s unity—federalism, the decentralization of govern-
ment. This method is, naturally, based on law, including
the strict regulation of the competence of the federal and
regional authorities. It is a method of not simply pre-
serving Russia’s integrity, but also ensuring the effective
protection of society and the citizens and the harmonious
development of the spiritual, cultural, and economic links
between all the components of the Russian state.

The majority of Russia’s citizens today understand that the
problem of Russia’s sovereignty is firmly linked to the
federalization of the country, and that federalism is the
surest path, not to undermining the integrity of the state, but
to promoting its cohesion. It is federalism that offers an
‘ outlet for the territorial communities’ desire for autonomy
and free development.

The first steps toward the formation of true federalism, in
particular the redistribution of power functions from the
center to the regions, were perceived by many as a sign of
the state’s weakness, an encroachment on its sovereignty,
and even a threat to its territorial integrity. It must be
admitted that there were some grounds for such fears. At
first, there were politicians who built their careers specif-
ically on separatist slogans and on the struggle against the
federal authority.

But federalism in Russia gained strength, and by last year
it had become clear that the overt threat of separatism had
been overcome. This is considerably to the credit of both
the federal and the territorial authorities. They did not
allow the situation to become really dangerous to Russia’s
integrity. With very rare exceptions, the question of any
territories’ seceding from Russia did not arise. We had
sufficient statesmanlike wisdom and responsibility for
this.

Treaties between the Russian Federation organs of state

power and the organs of power of Tatarstan, Bashkor-
tostan, and Kabardino-Balkaria made it possible to raise

the level of mutual understanding with those components
of the Federation and to stabilize the political situation
there.

The time has come to formulate and enshrine in legislation
the criteria on the basis of which the federal organs of
power can clarify constitutional relations with the Feder-
ation components. Here the constitutional principle of our
Federation’s structure must remain inviolate.

The Federation components have enormous scope for total
self-fulfillment within the framework of the Russian Con-
stitution. It guarantees them the broadest powers to
resolve ther own internal problems. They retain all the
rights of state power except those listed as federal prerog-
atives. In order to reflect the peculiar characteristics of life
in their own territories, they are entitled to create their
own fundamental laws—charters and constitutions.

Nonetheless the granting to the Federation components of
wide autonomy and the delimitation of spheres of juris-
diction and powers between the federal organs of power
and the organs of power of individual Federation compo-
nents do not entirely solve the problem of preserving the
unity and integrity of the Russian state. It is necessary to
improve regional policy. This is the most important area of
federal policy, based on an integrated system of documents
reflecting both the strategic national security interests of
Russia as a unified federal state, and the developmental
needs of the regions themselves, and—most important—
the need of all the Federation components for coordinated
cooperation with one another for the benefit of each
individual territory and the country as a whole.

Experience shows that the interests of an individual terri-
tory by no means always coincide with the interests of the
state as a whole, and are not infrequently i in conflict with
the interests of neighboring territories.

But despite these complexities and contradictions, last
year it proved possible to make marked progress in the
formulation of regional policy. This problem has been
studied conceptually both in the presidential structures,
and in the government, and in the Federal Assembly. It is
now a matter of translating concepts into the language of
concrete measures and programs. This work should be
completed by the fall of this year, in order to provide for
the appropriate measures in next year’s budget.

Last year was the year of the birth of Russian budgetary
federalism. Unified normatives were formulated for the
distribution of revenue from the main federal taxes
between the federal and regional budgets, and a formula
was defined for the expenditure of resources from the
Fund for Financial Support for the Federation Compo-
nents. As a result the budgetary autonomy and responsi-
bility of the Federation components increased, and it was
possible to a significant extent to relax the tension in
interbudgetary relations.

In 1995 a new mechanism for budgetary relationships
should be established, embracing all the Federation com-
ponents. The granting of federal support funds to the
territories should be substantiated to a greater degree.

The reason why many democracies perished was the
inability of the people to realize [the need for] and organize
a force for the defense of their right and to embody their
collective will into an effective state power.

The source of sovereign power in the Russian Federation is
its multinational people. The people alone are empowered
to either directly exercise state power by, for example,
adopting decisions through a referendum, or by dclegating
the implementation of their will to organs of state power
and local self-government.

There is no greater power than state power within the
state’s parameters: Its legal authority is greater than the
authority of any other power, and its behests have supreme
legal effect.

Thus, in order to ensure sovereignty, the institutions of
state power have the right not only to rely on their
moral-legal authority but also, in certain circumstances, to

affirm this authority through state coercion. This is a
necessary element of democratic legal order.

Whenever state organs use coercion as one of the means for
exercising the functions of the state, they must be guided
by the will of the people which, in its turn, is expressed
primarily in the Constitution.

In parallel with sovereignty and independence, state integ-
rity is one of the main constitutional values. If a state is
unwilling or unable to maintain its integrity, it also loses its
sovereignty. Its integrated fabric falls apart, and this is
followed by the disintegration of society. Chaos emerges,
and it leads to total violence.

State integrity incorporates several fundamental elements.
State power has no right to tolerate any encroachment
against any of these.

1. Unity of the state’s civic organism. Each citizen is part
of a unified whole—the people—and is linked to them by
a rather rigid bond defined by the idea of citizenship.

The unity of the civic organism, inseparably linked to the
state, also testifies to the unity of the state itself.

The exclusion of one part of the people from the common
civic organism spiritually and materially impoverishes
both the part in question and the people as a whole. Even
if such an action were possible, it could only be taken
legally and subject to the existence of special conditions.

2. Unity of the property complex. The common wealth has
been accumulated by many generations of the people. It is
the product of property ard personal sacrifices already
made or still being made by the whole people. Breaking the
integrity of the state means undermining the material
foundation of the people’s existence.

3. Unity of power. Sovereign power in any state is unified.
This does not at all contradict the principle of the separa-
tion of powers since, in compliance with this principle,
only the functions and powers of state institutions are
separated. Such a construction of power is intended not to
destroy the state machinery but only to ensure that this
machinery functions effectively and to prevent the emer-
gence of any threat of fatal monopolization of power.

4. Integrity of territory. The meaning of this concept is
much broader than normally imagined. Territorial integ-
rity would hardly have been at the focus of so much
attention had it involved only space and resources.

The loss of the part of the people inhabiting the territory
being torn away is as damaging to the state as, for example,
the loss of a person’s hand. For this very reason, any
actions aimed at tearing away a part of the state territory
must be deemed crimes against the state as a whole.

1.4. Outlines of Reform in the Defense and Security

The state of affairs in the military sphere at the present
stage is characterized by a considerable lessening of the
immediate threat of direct military aggression against
Russia. The Russian Federation does not regard a single

associations, including in the security sphere.

The task of the first years of reform was to create the

In continuing to resolve that task we must move further.

function effectively.

Our country’s culture ensures the integrity of the nations

Russia well knows what the law of force is. Only it has to
become aware of the force of law.

A new awareness of the law is only just being shaped in
citizens. This has largely been conditioned not only by
historical traditions but also by the modus operandi of the
institutions of power and by the legal awareness of the
people who represent them. A dangerous phenomenon for
our development is thereby brewing: The rights of the
individual, which have never in Russian history been
regarded as a practical state priority, run the risk of
remaining declarative. The authorities will continue to
mention them in official documents, while the citizen will
continue to feel his own lack of legal protection. In other
words, the danger lies in the discreditation of the concept
of “human rights” in social and political practice and,
thus, in the discreditation of the very essence of the
Russian Constitution.

Our state is still not so rich that it can materially ensure all
constitutional human and civil rights and freedoms
without exception at the highest level. Minimum state
standards have not yet been formulated legislatively. But
even when this happens, their actual substance will hardly
satisfy at once the expectations of a high quality of life.
Openness and honesty are most important in this sphere.
People suffer far more from unwarranted promises, par-
ticularly at election time, than from an objective assess-
ment of reality.

Practice shows, however, that even those rights and free-
doms which do not require excessive financial backup are
far from always reliably protected.

A citizen has the right to demand that the state ensure his
personal safety—protection of life, health, dignity, and
property. The state is still coping unsatisfactorily with this
duty.

and reliable law and order. A democratic state and a

3.2. Formation of the Judiciary

The most specific part of the state system for the protec-
tion of the law—the judiciary—is a full-fledged branch of
the state authorities. It has played and is continuing to play
a leading role in asserting the principle of the supremacy of
the law in modern civilization. But this specific feature has
been fully recognized neither in the state apparatus nor in
society.

Let us be frank. We often still see the judicial system as a
peripheral state institution. We forget that without a
full-fledged judiciary the remaining branches of state
power are ineffective, so the state system as a whole will
remain unstable.

The sluggish judicial reform, the demeaning working con-
ditions that still exist in courts, the absence of an integral
legal base for the organization and activities of the judicial
system, an inadequate number of judges—all these show
an ignorance of the vital importance of courts to the
normal life of society and the state.

Criticism could also be leveled at the judges themselves. In
particular, it is no secret that by no means all judges are
prepared to accept the altered principles of law and some-
times show their political predilections when examining
cases. But this does not give grounds for postponing the
actions of the legislative and executive branches.

All the more so as there is a basis in constitutional law for

First, presidential edicts are acts issued not by the head of
the executive, but by the head of state. In other words,
their absolutely law-governed [podzakonnyy] nature is not

Second, one of the president’s constitutionally established
prerogatives is to determine the main avenues of the
country’s domestic and foreign policy. It is clear that it is
by no means only the president’s annual message that
should be considered a way of implementing this power.

Third, the need to implement the function of guarantor of
the Constitution also makes it possible for the president to

issue normative edicts to ensure the operation of the
Russian Federation Constitution.

Thus, until the relevant laws are in place, presidential
edicts remain a full-fledged legal basis for the emergence,

3.3. State Power and Social Institutions

The effectiveness of power in a democratic state depends
not least on the quality of its interaction with civil society.

Over the past few years new institutions of civil society
have been dynamically taking shape. More than 2,000
voluntary associations have appeared at the all-Russia
level alone, not to mention local initiatives. These include
social protection organizations, charitable associations,
environmental movements, and social organizations in the
sphere of science, culture, and education. The process of
creating political parties and movements is rapidly under
way. Professional associations are being transformed.

The state’s duty with regard to civil society is to promote its
formation and full-fledged functioning.

The task of providing state support for the institutions of a
civil society was described in last year’s presidential mes-
sage to the Federal Assembly as an investment in the
stability of a democratic state itself. This is our long-term
goal. The progress toward it over the past year was insig-
nificant.

As a result, there has been a lack of constructive interac-
tion between social and state institutions. The inefficiency
of state organs which are slowly getting to grips with their
new functions under changing circumstances has had an
effect, as has the inadequacy of the constructive efforts

3.4. The Mass Media and Power

Relations between the mass media and the state institu-
tions of power have become a key issue of Russia’s
political life.

The Russian journalists’ union assessed the past year of
1994 as a period of the aggravation of relations between
the country’s power structures and the mass media. This
confrontation has underlying historical reasons. There are
also transient, political reasons.

The mass media have made a tremendous contribution to
the struggle against totalitarianism. In the late eighties
there was a struggle with state censorship for verbal and
written freedom of speech. The first to make this break-
through were the mass media. With the support of all
society. But only journalists received the right to informa-
tion while other citizens received the right to information
via the mass media. This privileged position for journalists
remains to this day.

Having won freedom for mass information the mass media
became the first political opposition in the country repre-
senting a real independent force. This was justified and
useful, although according to the canons of journalism that
is not how it should be. But later, when a party political
opposition had arisen and strengthened in Russia the mass
did not renounce their acquired but not inherent
unction.

It must be admitted that the reasons which intensified the
confrontation which had emerged included some actions
by the authorities. For instance, the habit, inherited by
some parliamentarians from Soviet times, of instructing
“the mirror of social life’ —the mass media—as to how the
reflection in that mirror should look. A number of draft

laws in the sphere of regulating the mass media are
conceptualiy rejected by journalists. Often the situation is

The desire to subordinate the mass media can often be
seen in the actions of the executive branch too. Together
with methods of political and economic pressure there are
known instances of preaching to journalists and of direct
pressure on them and of insults to the inconvenient ones.
Matters reached the point of physical pressure. This is
borne out, for instance, by the materials of the foundation
for the defense of glasnost relating to persecutions of
journalists, especially the foundation's recent report on the
persecution of journalists and the press in the period of the
Chechen events.

The “fourth estate” has not remained in debt in the “war
of nerves” which has been played out. It repays the
legislative and executive branches with interest for their
obvious and alleged sins. The tone of calm dialogue has
been lost. As a rule the conversation is conducted in the
language of diktat and ultimatums. Accusing others of
violating legality, the mass media themselves often go
beyond the legal framework.

The situation in which organs of power and the mass
media have placed themselves is no longer tolerable. The
way out is via compliance with the law and a common
interest in overcoming confrontation.

The constitutional principle of freedom of mass informa-
tion is immutable. Without this a democratic society
cannot exist. The truthful word of the journalist is some-
times the last barrier to tyranny by officials, bureaucratic
raging, the covering up of social ills, or deliberate disinfor-
mation.

Journalists’ complete access to all socially significant infor-
mation, openness and glasnost in the work of organs of
power (with legally prescribed exceptions, of course, linked
with state secrets and special methods of combatting
crime) must be ensured. Informational openness must
indeed be central to state policy in the field of the mass
media, serve constructiveness, and reveal the real objec-
tives and motives for the activity ox all branches of power.

State information policy must also ensure firm support
and protection for journalists. Journalism has indeed
become a dangerous profession. Journalists must be pro-
tected from any kind of pressure, especially abuse and
physical reprisals.

It is the civic and professional duty of journalists to
promote the spiritual and moral rebirth of Russia and the
strengthening of the democratic foundations of its state-
hood.

Concern for the moral state of society, primarily the moral
health of young people, is a matter of special importance.
Journalists’ moral stance could also extend to the use of
unscrupulous advertising and the violence, baseness, and

Conclusion

Proclaiming the strengthening of institutions of power to
be a priority task is not equivalent to strengthening the
omnipotence of the bureaucratic regime. On the contrary,
for the first time in our history we are striving to create in
Russia a genuinely democratic, rule-of-law state. This will
emerge only if a full-fledged civil society is formed in our
country. This is a dual task, and its resolution requires the
common efforts of all genuine patriots of Russia.

While laying the foundations of a new, democratic state
system, the Constitution has still not started working to its
full capacity. We can no longer defer the implementation
of the package of measures to improve the effectiveness of
state power mentioned in the Message.

First—the securing in practice of Russians’ rights and
freedoms. A society in which a person does not have

CENTRAL EURASIA 31

political, economic, and social guarantees is incapable of
resisting extremist demagoguery and recurrences of total-
itarianism.

Second—the creation of an effective system of interaction
among organs of executive, legislative, and judicial power.
Third—the ensuring of continuity in the democratic pro-
cess. Russia’s supreme organs of power have been formed
democratically. But democracy in our country will only
put down strong roots when the people’s inalienable right
to grant or withdraw a mandate for power by elections at
the times stipulated by the Constitution becomes the
norm.

Fourth—the implementation of the constitutional princi-
ples of federalism. Russia’s unity and integrity does not
infringe the rights of the components of the Federation.
What is needed is, on the one hand, precise delimitation of
the powers of federal organs of power and oblast, kray, and
republic organs of power and, on the other, real equality of
all the components of the Federation.

Fifth—the effective participation of the state in the eco-
nomic process. The well-conceived use of economic and
administrative instruments of state regulatior. will make it
possible not only to ensure social stability and the forma-
tion of potential for economic growth, but also to raise
Russia to front-line positions in the world economy.

Sixth—the ensuring of the effective functioning of the
national security system. This primarily concerns the cre-
ation of a mechanism for decisionmaking within the
national security sphere particularly in those cases where it
is necessary to activate power structures. A system for
coordinating the implementation of adopted decisions by
all state structures is also equally important. Lastly, it is
necessary to resolutely embark on the implementation of
military reform, clearly laying down its priorities and
parameters and eschewing ill-conceived decisions and
improvisation.

Seventh—the definition of the Russian state’s position in
the world arena in line with our national interests. A great
deal will be determined by the nature of our ties with the
CIS states. On the basis of close mutually beneficial
cooperation with them we will be able to develop our
relations with the far abroad and articulate our approach
to the resolution of key international problems.

The resolution of these tasks can only be ensured by joint
actions on the part of all constructive political forces which
hold dear the future of Russia in its striving for prosperity
and democracy.
