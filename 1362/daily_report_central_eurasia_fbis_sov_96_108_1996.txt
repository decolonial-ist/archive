Regional Papers Suggest NE DAY BOG Circulation
May "Backfire" on Yeltsin Regional media continue to
censure the anti-communist newspaper supplement NE
DAY BOG, which is published by Yeltsin's support
group in Moscow, and to criticize Yeltsin's support
groups for forcing anti-communist propaganda on the
voting population. SOVETSKAYA SIBIR (29 May)
contended ironically that NE DAY BOG has in fact
succeeded in breaking voter apathy toward politics.
Now, voters “argue until they get hoarse,” but many
agree that NE DAY BOG has “backfired” on the
Yeltsin campaign. NIZHEGORODSKAYA PRAVDA
(25 April) reported that its editorial office had been
“battered” with a “squall of telephone calls” in protest
after NE DAY BOG was surreptitiously distributed
with NIZHEGORODSKAYA PRAVDA. The paper
reportedly requested that the local prosecutor launch an
investigation of the incident.

INTERFAX (27 May) reported that North Ossetian post
offices stopped delivering NE DAY BOG. The move
was a response to recommendations from the local
electoral commission, which concluded that the post
office's distribution of the supplement violated electoral
laws that prohibit government-run organizations from
conducting election campaigning.

{FBIS Translated Text} Russian President Boris Yeltsin
has completed his trip to Perm. As during the pre-
vious May visits to the regions he performed two

state. In Perm Boris Nikolayevich submitted his Ac-

day in ROSSTYSKIYE VESTI) and signed a tripartite
agreement with Perm Oblast and Komi-Permyak Au-
tonomous Okrug leaders on the demarcation of powers
and areas of jurisdiction.

Addressing the city’s public at Perm’s Cultural and
Business Center yesterday, Boris Yeltsin outlined the
main features of his election program. The president
said that he completed work on it on Monday 27 May
and signed it the same day. “My program is not a matter
of election promises. It is a report on what has been
achieved and a specific plan for upcom'ng work. My
hard experience gives me the right and the confidence
to say that this program will be implemented'”

“You will not find vain promises or fine slogans in my
program,” the head of state said. “It details everything
that has been accomplished over the past five years and

justice minister or minister of propaganda, which would

Moreover, according to certain information from the

in Paris six years ago is hanging by a thread. Yet that
document was meant to be and in principle could serve
as a basis for a stable system of international relations
on an almost Europe-wide scale.

The treaty was drawn up under the conditions of
confrontation between two military-political alliances
— NATO and the Warsaw Pact — and the security
model enshrined in it was designed to maintain the
balance between them. But history does not stand still.
And although 23 states took part in drawing up the
document, 22 signed it — by then Germany had been
unified. Incidentally, it has to be said that the Germans
then took on additional commitments to reduce their
conventional arms, and so there was no particular
distortion to the blocs’ security levels. But then the
Warsaw Pact and the USSR collapsed.

A fundamentally new situation had come about in which
the security interests of the states taking part in military-
political alliances and of states not taking part in them
were not equally considered. Take just the so-called
“cascade” principle, which made it possible to “transfer”
a quota for any kind of conventional arms from one bloc
participant to another. Put crudely, any NATO country
is able to concentrate up to 13,300 tanks on its territory
by using its allies’ quotas, whereas Russia can have
no more than 6,400. Despite this, the 1992 Tashkent
meeting of eight independent republics of the former
USSR adopted a decision to implement the treaty in
the form in which it was signed. Commitments were
redistributed as well. The lion’s share of the cuts, of
course, went to Russia.

What were we guided by in saving a treaty that was now
disadvantageous for us? First and foremost, by a rejec

tion of confrontation with the West and the strength-
ening of confidence in each other. It is undoubtedly to
the treaty’s merit that mutual inspections and advance
mutual notification from countries about conventional
armed forces activity have become possible. But the
main motive force has been and continues to be the hope
that a security system will be created in Europe which
would protect the interests of all states. But the decision
to expand NATO eastward makes our participation in
this treaty in its present form barely acceptable, since a
destabilizing military potential could take shape beside
our borders. Moreover, the restrictions contained in the
treaty take no account of the need to avert local con-
flicts — as has been graphically demonstrated by events
in Chechnya. There is also the “flank problem.” Russia
has taken on all the USSR’s commitments to bring its
flanks armaments into line with the treaty. As a result
we are now unable to build any kind of sensible military

liaments — it is a joint legislative organ in which all
parties have equal rights. Essentially, it is a suprana-
tional parliament of the “union of four” — Belarus,
Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, and the Russian Federation —
agreement on which was signed in Bishkek 28 May.

Kt is quite clear that a common iegislative base —
without which the cconomic union will not work
and cooperation in the sphere of culture, science,
education, and so forth will remain just good intentions
— is needed for joint work and to develop relations.
The creation of a legal basis will be handled by
the committee, whose elected chairman is Almambet
Matubraimov, speaker of the upper house of the Kyrgyz
parliament.

Akayev put it, trade will revive and a free common

(FBIS Translated Text] Mstislav Afanasyev, first deputy
head of the Working Center of Economic Reforms at
the Russian Federation government and the executive
secretary of the commission on nonpayments, said in
an interview with SEGODNYA that the growth of
nonpayments in the Russian economy continues. In
April, nonpayments to the federal budget rose by 21.8
percent and, therefore, amounted to 58.9 trillion rubles.
In addition to a “quite predictable” growth of arrears in
anticipation of the upcoming election, arrears of wages
at private enterprises have also grown in April. At the
same time, the state itself has virtually settled all of its
debts that earlier accounted for about 19 percent of the
entire arrears in wages (about 3 billion rubles).

So-called classic nonpayments; i.e., those for enterprise
deliveries, were also observed to rise from traditional
3 percent of GDP to 4.8 percent. As for the entire
overdue credit indebtedness of enterprises, in April it
amounted to 14.1 percent of GDP, which, over the past
18 months, is a record high figure. This notwithstanding,
the government estimates that the total number of
enterprises that fail to pay wages is presently about
48,000; i.c., less than 2 percent of all the registered
legal bodies in Russia. Despite this “not very jubilant
news,” however, the expert noted a trend towards a rise
in the industrial production index over the past several
months, calling it "the first signs of economic: growth.”

Mr. Afanasyev believes that the nonpayments growth
trend has more than one cause. The primary — “the
most fundamental and decisive” — factor is that of the
forthcoming presidential election. The behavior of the
government, enterprise directors, and bankers is being
determined now by the anticipation of the presidential
election results. A high degree of political and economic
risk, as weil as a decline in the trust that the market
components have towards one another, leads both to
the rise in debts to the budget and to the growth of

nonpayments for deliveries.

The second cause — which is, according to the expert,
a direct consequence of the first one — is a “rational
behavior” of directors, who have become aware of the
inability, or possibly unwillingness, of the authorities

RUSSIA NATIONAL AFFAIRS 37

to improve the performance of the state apparatus. In
Mr. Afanasyev’s opinion, the actions of the government
(such as the edicts on the restructuring of enterprise
debts) have given rise to “generally well-justified expec-
tations on the part of the directors” that no one will touch
them before the final election results are known, as well
as later. “If Gennadiy Zyuganov comes to power, the
time for quite a different economy will come, where the
nonpayment issue will be buried by other, much more
significant problems,” the expert believes. “If, however,
Boris Yeltsin wins, the directors hope that the authori-
ties will forgive enterprises their debts." Mr. Afanasyev
said, however, that these expectations are only partially
justified, since the issue of nonpayments is a burning
problem and the state machinery is sure to set up a so-
lution with all its might.

This is partly evidenced by the changes in the nonpay-
ments structure, showing — despite the general growth
in nonpayments — that the share of debt to the bud-
get tended to decrease in the past two months. Mr.
Afanasyev himself is attributing this process to the hard-
ening of the position of the tax bodies with respect to
major defaulters.

also show quite interesting trends. Whereas earlier the
main defaulters were found mainly in raw material
sectors — such as oil, gas production, and metallurgy
— now enterprises of the energy, defense industry and
machine-building sectors come to the foreground. It can
be supposed that nonpayments are currently suggestive
of the political and economic preferences of the Russian
directors’ corps. The financial (in the form of duly
paid taxes) support of the authorities is now becoming

(FBIS Translated Text] According to Sven Jurgenson,
director-general of the political department at the Min-
istry of Foreign Affairs, the planned changes to the Con-
venation on Armed Forces in Europe (CFE] Treaty will
harm Estonian security interests. Jurgenson noted that
under the agreement reached by Russia and the United
States on Saturday the ceiling on weapon deployment
in Pskov region [adjoining Estonia) will no longer be
valid. For example, instead of the present 180 armored
carriers Russia will be allowed to keep 600 in Pskov re-
gion, despite the fact that the representatives of the U.S.
Department of State previously assured Estonia that the
ceiling on armored carriers in Pskov region will not ex-
ceed 380.

During the talks held in Vienna Russia requested an
increase in the maximum armaments stipulated by
the 1990 CFE Treaty on its Western border and the
Caucasus area. Moscow gave its reasons as losing allies
in Eastern Europe and the country having lost territory
after the CFE Treaty was concluded.
