Russia: Yeltsin Advised Against Promising Early
End to Chechen War

MK2903101496 Moscow SEGODNYA in Russian
28 Mar 96 pl

[Report by Dmitriy Volkov under the “Plans” rubric:
“Television Program for Sunday. Boris Yeltsin Will Do
His Utmost for Chechnya, Even Though This May Not
Be Much"]

[FBIS Translated Text] Boris Yeltsin is presented with
an immensely difficult dilemma — he promised to make
a televised address to Russian citizens on 31 March to
reveal the plan for a peaceful settlement of the Chechen
conflict. Such a plan cannot contain any miraculous
recipes for resolving this problem, but the president has
to go on the air all the same one he has promised to
do so. It is clear what the president can ofier. This is
the conclusion of a power-sharing treaty with Chechnya,
modeled on a number of similar treaties on the delimita-
tion of power with autonomous republics that have been
successfully operating over the past several years; this is
possibly the confirmation of Doku Zavgayev's authority
by holding elections for Chechnya’s legislature; this is
couunuing efforts to sign peace treaties with the elders
of Chechen villages for the purpose of multiplying the
safe havens, which a Kremlin source called “islands of
peace” (and — by implication — the continuation of
attempts to find influential partners for negotiations ca-
pable of compromise); and, finally, the continuing use of
force, and that not only in Chechnya, but also in neigh-
boring North Caucasus republics, including Dagestan
and Ingushetia. The trouble is that this is not what the
audience wants to hear from the president; it will ex-
pect him to deliver a trite and meaningless promise —
such as to stop the killings of servicemen of the In-
ternal Affairs Ministry and Defense Ministry troops in
Chechnya, and also, for the sake of quieting their con-
science, to stop casualties among the civilian population
of the republic — but at the same time to quash Du-
dayev’s mutiny and to end the war victoriously. It is
impossible to fulfill it all at one stroke. It seems that
the president's objective in this situation should not be
a public announcement of his Chechen campaign plan
(insofar as this plan cannot be figured out it should be
deemed to be secret and not subject to publication via
television), but a convincing appeal to the nation, sim-
ilar to that delivered by the initially bewildered Soviet
leadership after Nazi Germany's invasion of the USSR
— “our cause is just, and we shall win.” At the same
time, the most sensitive issue is the command's ability
to achieve a victory with as little bloodletting as pos-
sible. No one has any illusions as to the strategic and
tactical talents of Pavel Grachev. current Russian de-
fense minister. but the replacement of this commander

will only do any good if its results can be predicted, at
least to some extent. The absence of a realistic plan for
pacifying Chechnya can probably also be seen as a posi-
tive factor, at least given the results achieved by diverse
plans for taking Groznyy with one single paratroops
regiment or the Chechen people's rising in arms against
Dzhokhar Dudayev, the illegitimate president of Chech-
nya. Perhaps the strongest argument against deliberate
attempts at planning in Russian politics is the bloody
and confused Chechen campaign itself, in which virtu-
ally all successes that have been achieved by the federal
forces came despite the intentions of their generals and
geopoliticians, not thanks to them. In fact, searly the
sole mistake made by the Kremlin is the identification
of the government with the Chechen war. It is not Boris
Yeltsin who invented the problems Russia has encoun-
tered in the North Caucasus. He, being a responsible
head of state, may not ignore these problems, nor can
he avoid attempts to resolve them. On the contrary, any
of the incumbent president's rivals attempting to win
votes by drawing attention to Russia’s actual difficul-
ties in the Caucasus make themselves appear in a fairly
unfavorable light when they pass Russian problems off
as those of the president. If the president's determina-
tion were enough to pacify Chechnya (incidentally, the
guerrilla war did not stop in Chechnya in the 1930's or
the 1940's) it would all have ended long ago. Hence, the
critics are bending the truth. Unfortunately, the Chechen
problem will remain with us for a long time to come,
and linking it to the electoral interests of the key rivals
in the race for the Kremlin office would be cynical, to
say the least.

[Report by Yuliya Kalinina: “Peace Is Inevitable.
The Chechens Have Received an Offer They Cannot

{FBIS Translated Text) On Tuesday villages in
Achkhoy-Martan Rayon were preparing for the signing
of a peace agreement. Villagers gathered in their central
squares and listened to speeches by deputies from the
Zavgayev government and the Russian military.

"A peace process is under way here,” a policeman
told me meaningfully. And then he turned to the roar
behind his back. Five kilometers from there, rockets
were pounding old Achkhoy; explosions flared up
magnificently with mushrooms of black smoke rising
to the sky.

Nearly all the villages in the mountain areas of Chech-
nya have already signed or agreed to sign a cease-fire.
Fewer than ten resist the agreement. The agreement it-
self contains the following demands for the residents:
surrender a certain number of weapons, recognize the
Zavgayev government, and allow interior troops into the
village to check passports and search houses.

Usually, several days before the formal act of signing
the outskirts of a particular village are methodically
subjected to artillery and aviation strikes, which reduces
its residents to a state of acquiescence and submission
to fate. They are ready to sign just about anything to
stop the nightmare. Villages bordering the “protected”
populated areas need no further persuasion. Samashki 1s
the most graphic example. Incidentally, to date, nobody
is allowed into the village. Refugees who broke out of
Samashki three days ago explain with tears in their eyes
that they must bury their relatives. Refugees say not less
than 500 people were killed in Samashki last week.

It should be noted for the sake of objectivity that after
the signing of the peace agreements the Chechens’
attitude toward the federal troops or Zavgayev does not
get any warmer. They admit that they sign the agreement
because they have to, not because it is fully responsive
to their positions.

Villagers are having the biggest problem with the
surrender of weapons. Normally, federal commanders
present a village with an item:zed list — for example
27 automatic rifles, two machine guns, seven grenade
launchers, and 10 mines. The strange part is that nobody
knows where these figures come from, why the federals

‘Alliance of Four’

[Analysis by Elmar Murtazayev: “Russia Prepares Base
for ‘Alliance of Four’"]}

(FBIS Translated Text] An agreement has been drawn
up in the Russian government on deeper economic, sci-
. tific and cultural integration between Russia, Belarus,
Kazakhstan, and Kyrgyzstan. The document is expected
to be signed by the heads of these states at the end of
March or early in April.

The document calls for creating a single customs
territory, unified procedures of investment activities,
buying real estate, single methods for forming prices
of products of natural monopolies. sautual convertibility
of national currencies, and an agreement on authorized
banks. It also states an intention “to proceed toward
a single currency.” Furthermore, supranational bodies
are to be formed to which members of the union
will delegate powers to make “economic and legal
decisions.” This agreement is being drawn up in a
total news blackout by, according to some information,
Russian presidential structures with Minsotrudnichestvo
{Ministry on Economic Cooperation With Member-
States of the Commonwealth] assistance.

The signing of the agreement will legally formalize
the existing economic divide between the countries
of the Commonwealth. [as published) Talks late last
November with Uzbekistan, which earlier announced
plans to join the Customs Union, became deadlociesd,
meaning that this Central Asian country will hardly be
joining the single customs zone. On the other hand,
Kyrgyzstan, which is not a Customs Union member yet,
is obliged to join it before signing the documents on
greater integration. Askar Akayev, who is in Moscow
now, will probably sign the Customs Union agreement
today so as to enable a joint statement on “a new stage
of integration.”

However, members of the “alliance of four” are in the
future going to face several considerable problems at
once. First, unresolved as yet is the question of the rela-
tionship Cetween representatives of the countries partic-
ipating in the agreement and the size of their financial
contributions toward the [planned] supranational bod-
ies. Second, some points in the agreement necessitate
amendmenis to the constitutions of the parties to the
agreement (one such point is a single customs terri-
tory). Third, according to some drafters of the agree-
ment. Belarus’ attempts to hasten integration processes
under bilateral understandings [between Russia and Be-
