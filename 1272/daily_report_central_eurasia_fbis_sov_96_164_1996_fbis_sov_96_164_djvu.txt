{FBIS Trans!ated Text] The long and tangled story of
the escape of seven Russian pilots — the crew of an
ll-76 aircraft operated by the “Aecrostan" siztine — 1s
almost a ready plot for a gripping thriller. Although
many significant aspects of the escape itself and the
preparations for it are being carefully withheld by
both Russian officials and the pilots themselves, certain
details of the escape that KOMMERSANT-DAILY has
learned make it possible to deduce the most likely
picture of the goings-on involving the captive crew. But
our conclusions are only a theory, attempting to explain
the happy ending of this dramatically charged story.

It is small secret that since the very beginning of the
saga of the Il-76 crew’s captivity by the Talibs the Rus-
sian special services were ready to carry out a special
operation to rescue the pilots by force. But political
agencies, first of all, the Frreign Ministry, convinced
the Kremlin that the conflict could be resolved through
their channels. However, as early as December 1995
it became obvious that behind the Talibs’ promises to
release the crew members, who had been in captivity
for the fifth month by that time, there was but a thinly
veiled desire by the Talibs (and certain powers standing
behind them) to turn the pilots into a lever of constant
pressure on Russia with a view to removing it from ef-
forts to resolve the tight knot of regional problems.

It was precisely then, in late 1995-early 1996, that
threatening statements by some politicians and even
diplomats emerged in the Russian press regarding the
likelihood of using “other,” quite undiplomatic methods
of resolving the problems of rescuing the Russian pilcts.
According to KOMMERSANT-DAILY’s information,
at the time Moscow in ftct started preparations for
a commando operation — a number of corresponding
subunits of certain special services were involved in
designing a rescue plan. By some accounts, the bulk
of the task force was to have been made up of
commandos of the Main Inte’digence Directorate [GRU]
special-purpose units. But soon the mood of certain
hopelessness regafding Use Kandahar problem made
room for a new flurry of Moscow's diplomatic activity.
The plan for carrying out a commando operation was
frozen. Yet, even if it had been 100-percent ready, there
would have been no guarantee tha: it could be carried
out without casualties, particularly among the captive
crew members, who were always carefully guarded.

Consequently, the Foreign Ministry and the Foreign In-

pay ransom for the crew was being prepared together

they started devising an escape plan almost right after
they were forced to land in Kandahar a year ago, it could
become a reality only with outside assistance, and con-
siderable assistance at that.

Such assistance (in fact all conceivable assistance under
the circumstances) was not found wanting. First, the
safest route and flight regime for 11-76 — through Iran
to the United Arab Emirates, not through Pakistan to
India, where the Talibs were actually looking for the
aircraft — was chosen and agreed upon at the highest
level. In the meantime, as soon as the plane took
off from Kandahar, it was given the requisite flight
documents — a permit number and a flight number
to fly over the territories of Iran and the United Arab
Emirates — without any delays and formalities through
the Sharja office of the “Transavia” airline. It is quite
possible that during a meeting of the crew members
with a “Transavia” representative (he regularly brought
them food and water) they were given the requisite
information on the solely possible method of escaping
an inevitable pu-suit — to fly at an altitude of up to 100
meters and at a speed of up to 400 km/h with complete
radio silence.

The only thing the crew could afford was to establish
short-wave communications with Sharja, and that only
in order to obtain flight documents. Surprisingly, the
crew received an immediate response and flight data
(which obviously must have been agreed upon with
Tehran in advance) as soon as it went on the air. This
