Bread, Grain Shortages in CIS

LD1I912112594 Moscow ITAR-TASS in English 1101
GMT 19 Dec 94

[By ITAR-TASS Correspondent Pyotor Tsyren-
dorzhiyev]

[FBIS Transcribed Text] December 19 TASS - A 35-40
per cent price rise on bread is possible in Buryatia in near
future, ITAR- TASS learned on Monday from the «ress
service of the Buryatian president and government. The
expected prise rise is connected with the increased cost
of transportation and electricity. So far a loaf of bread
costs 850 roubles. Bread is the main food for more than
half of the republic’s population.

eae

Russia experiences difficulties with grain procurement.
Approximately 16-17 million tonnes of grain are now
kept in state-owned granaries, ten million tonnes less
than last year. The price of the Orenburg strain of wheat
(the most expensive, Durum wheat) is 220 thousand
roubles per tonne, which is half the price for Durum
wheat, for instance, in France (150 dollars).

Regions try to protect their markets and to limit the sale
of grain to the state, and this complicates the situation
still more. A few days ago the Altay territory stopped
selling food grain in connection with an acute shortage of
grain in the local market. Quite possibly, the price of
bread in the Altai territory may rise 50 per cent.

Bread and cereals are not on sale in shops in the Kursk
and Bryansk regions, particularly on the border with
Ukraine. Because of steep price rises in Ukraine, by up to
1,000 per cent, Ukrainian citizens buy up large amounts
of food in Russian regions bordering on Ukraine.

On the whole, price rises on food products took place in
virtually all ex-USSR countries.

There are shortages of bread in Tajikistan, where the
majority +f large families rely on bread as their main
food. In Dushanbe, bread lines form from the evenings.
One person is not permitted to buy more than three
loaves of bread.

Bread nrczs in Belarus rose 30 per cent in the past ten
days. In Azerbaijan, bread prices went up by approxi-
mately 25 per cent within a few days. In Armenia, bread
became much more expensive after prices were freed on
December |. There are bread shortages in Kazakhstan.
Prices of baked products went up there by 30 per cent,
and a price rise of 50-100 per cent is probable. Bread is
rationed in Georgia—300 grams per person a day.

Carter Mission ‘Last Chance of Avoiding an
All-Out War’

MM 1912163794 Moscow IZVESTIYA in Russian
20 Dec 94 p 3

[Report by Maksim Yusin: “Carter's Mission to the
Balkans”’]

[FBIS Translated Text] A new player has burst meteor-
like onto the scene in Balkan politics, overshadowing the
international mediators from the United Nations, the
EU [European Union], and the “contact group” who
appeared here during the years of the Bosnian war.
Jimmy Carter has arrived in besieged Sarajevo.

The former U.S. President's peacemaking mission is
beginning at a critical moment in the Bosnian drama,
when even the most die-hard optimists seem to be losing
hope of the possibility of a peaceful settlement of the
conflict and are talking increasingly loudly about the
need to withdraw the “blue helmets” from Bosnia.
Possibly Carter’s trip is the last chance of avoiding an
all-out war in the Balkans which would have catastrophic
consequences for all the warring sides.

Mediation in the solution of the most involved and
seemingly insoluble international crises has become
Carter’s main speciality since he left the White House in
January 1981, having been routed by Ronald Reagan in
the elections two months earlier. Carter’s four-year term
as president was marked by a series of diplomatic
failures, the most serious of which was the Islamic
revolution in Iran which had catastrophic consequences
for the United States.

Nevertheless, many Americans who adhere to left-wing
views still believe that Carter was one of the most
brilliant presidents in the country’s postwar history and
that the voters did not assess him on his merits. Be that
as it may, Carter’s foreign policy successes in recent
years and, in particular, his sensational breakthrough in
the talks with North Korea on the nuclear issue, attest to
the former president’s exceptional dinlomatic abilities.

Will Carter be able to repeat the “Pyongyang miracle”
and find a way out of the Bosnian impasse? The hope of
that, unfortunately, is not great. The former president's
meeting with Muslim leader Alija Izetbegovic in Sara-
jevo on Sunday [18 December] confirmed that the
Bosnian Government takes a skeptical view of Carter’s
mission, believing that it offers the Serbs the hope of
emerging from internaiional isolation and imposing
their terms on the Muslims and Croats. Much should be
clarified by Carter’s talks, scheduled for Monday [19
December], with Bosnian Serb leader Radovan
Karadzic, who invited the U.S. peacemaker to the Bal-
kans

Admittedly, Carter’s recent comments will hardly cause
delight to Karadzic and his supporters. The former
president has stated that the basis for settling the conflict
must be the plan for the territorial division of Bosnia

categorically opposed, as is well known.

Meanwhile, the situation on the Bosnian war front has

Fikret Abdic. Abdic’s detachments, equipped and armed

Bosnia: ‘Should Not Be Taken Seriously’

LD1912184394 Moscow INTERFAX in English 1445
GMT 19 Dec 94

[From the “Diplomatic Panorama” feature: reports by
diplomatic correspondents Aleksandr Korzun, Igor Por-
shnev, Yevgeniy Terekhov, and others]

{[FBIS Transcribed Text] Moscow, Dec 19 (Interfax}—
U.S. ex-President Jimmy Carter’s mediatory efforts to
settle the conflict in Bosnia and Herzegovina “should
not be taken seriously,” a source in the Russian embassy
in Zagreb told Interfax by telephone on Monday [19
December].

He expressed hope that the “American officials, too, give
preference to the unity of the Contact Group on Bosnia”
(represented by Russia, the U.S., Britain, France and
Germany—lInterfax). ““The more so, since the previous
*solo’ moves by U.S. representatives neither aroused any
enthusiasm in former Yugoslavia, nor promoted the
settlement in any way,” said the Russian diplomat.

Last Sunday, Carter held consultations with Croatian
president Franjo Tudjman in the Croatian capital, and
met with Prime Minister of the Bosnian Muslims Haris
Silajdzic, and with the U.N. secretary-general’s special
envoy in former Yugoslavia Asushi Akashi. On the same
day, Carter flew out to Sarajevo (the capital of Bosnia
and Herzegovina—Interfax) to continue the talks.

The Russian diplomat announced that a regular meeting
of the Contact Group would take place in Belgrade on
Tuesday. “‘The group has its own plan, recognized by at
least two of the three parties involved in the conflict, and
also by Zagreb and Belgrade. The Contact Group will
strictly observe this plan whatever the outcome of
Carter’s mission is,”’ said the Russian diplomat.

He also said that both Zagreb and Sarajevo are skeptical
about the former U.S. president’s mediatory efforts. In
their turn, representatives of the U.N. Protection Force
in former Yugoslavia view this mission as Carter's
personal affair and are ready to give him technical
assistance “‘as an attempt to reconcile the conflicting
parties.”

highest level means recognizing him as head of state.

introduced in Chechnya. Consequently, a territorial
administration in the Chechen Republic is unconstitu-
tional.

It is quite clear that Chechnya is being offered a regime

Why Do Customs Borders Exist?

After the republics gained their sovereignty and were
recognized by the world community, they were naturally
unable to affect each other’s imports and exports. The
mayhem of re-exporting began right across the former
Soviet Union, and two countries in particular, Russia
and Kazakhstan, have suffered as a result. That is why
Russia was the first to start demarcating borders and
brought in the institution of customs controls. We now
have a preliminary accord, and if the heads of states
approve the customs legislation and data in Alma Ata

procedures and data will then be passed on that basis.
This will in turn allow the so-called customs union
between CIS countries to come into being. Subsequently,
if each state, which is party to the agreement, assumes
the function of guarding the external borders of these
countries, the borders between these countries will be
removed.

Russia recognizes the Tartu Peace Treaty of 1920,
Estonia is prepared to open negotiations over certain
concessions on the disputed territories, Prime Minister
Andres Tarand told BNS on Saturday [17 December].

“The primary condition for concluding a new border pact
is that Russia again recognize the Tartu Peace Treaty as
the international treaty under which the Estonian state was
born,” Tarand said. “The sooner a new border pact with
Russia is concluded, the better for Estonia.”

In Tarand’s words, the possibility of a new border
agreement between Estonia and Russia was under dis-
cussion at his meeting on Friday with Finnish President
Martti Ahtisaari and Prime Minister Esko Aho.

An agreement with Russia would allow Estonia to press
ahead with its plans to join the European Union, Tarand
said.

The disputed territory concerns about 2,000 square kilo-
meters of land in the region of Pechory and beyond the
Narva River that belonged to Estonia under the 1920
Tartu Peace Treaty. After the Soviet Union annexed
Estonia in 1940 the land was handed over to Russia’s
Pskov and Leningrad regions. After Estonia renewed its
claims on the land, Russia began in August unilaterally
marking down the border along the temporary control line.

Russian President Boris Yeltsin visited the disputed
border last month and vowed Moscow would not give up
“one single centimeter” of land.

Panevezys regional chamber of industry and commerce,
the newspaper LIETUVOS RYTAS reported.

imported to the region is only 5 percent, as compared to
Germany’s 19 percent and Poland’s 25 percent.

Polish influence is also very strong in the field of culture,
Sileris said. In his opinion, Lithuania has only “done
quite well” in creating joint construction companies.

The diplomat said Lithuania must allow Russian transit
through its territory to Kaliningrad, adding that he
makes no distinction between military and non-military
transit.

Sileris said Poland’s statements that it will not seek to
redirect Russian military transit through its own terri-
tory should be regarded as “diplomatic games.” In his
words, Poland wants military transit because it is eco-
nomically beneficial. The diplomat said he already saw
maps showing how Russian transit would be conducted
through Poland.
