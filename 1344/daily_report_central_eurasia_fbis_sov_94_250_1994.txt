CIS Customs Union May Start Operating in
Early 1996

MM2812130394 Moscow ROSSIYSKAYA GAZETA in
Russian 27 Dec ©¢ First Edition p 3

[Article by Aleksandr Gavrilyuk: “Customs Transit.
Internal CIS Borders Should Not Hinder Economic
Integration”’]

[FBIS Translated Text] We would recall that back in
1992 a draft agreement on the principles of a customs
union was drawn up on the initiative of the Russian
State Customs Committee. It was discussed by the heads
of the Commonwealth countries.

On the basis of the assessment of Sergey Fedosov, head
of the State Customs Committee’s Foreign Relations
Administration, it is proposed to exercise customs con-
trols in respect of third countries only along the external
perimeter of the bord r of the agreement signatories. In
other words, loads which have been checked by, say,
Russian customs, should pass without let or hindrance
across the territory of all member states of the union
without any additional checks.

This supranational structure, as it were, should be enti-
tled to make decisions that are binding on all the
“signatories.” Of course, each customs department loses
its sovereignty to a certain extent within the framework
of the union. Nonetheless, the mechanism has already
been worked out in rough. Currently the role of such a
coordinating organ could be played by the Council of
Customs Service Leaders set up by decision of the CIS
heads of government in January 1994. It is already
functioning and is currently trying to resolve the main
problem—that of unifying customs legislation. Without
this it will be impossible, by definition, to create a
customs union. But it is in this area that the main
differences have been revealed.

The Ukrainian delegation stated that it is an associate
member of the Commonwealth and, on this basis,
cannot discuss—much less sign—any agreements. But
when the question of setting up unified customs legisla-
tion was raised, the Ukrainian representative demanded
that the following amendment be made: “Excluding
words from the preamble which do not [as published]
contravene these basic principles.” These apparently
insignificant words are profoundly important. If this
amendment were adopted, the Basic Principles of the
CIS States’ Customs Legislation would lose their main
function—they would no longer be supranational.

A second contradiction came to light—this time
prompted by Turkmenistan. Representatives of that
state agreed that the Basic Principles of Customs Legis-
lation had been elaborated in accordance with interna-
tional standards and take account of the national fea-
tures of all the future union’s participants. The only
thing that bothers them is that the customs services are
equated with law enforcement organs—even within the
limits of their authority. What do they mean?

INTERREGIONAL AFFAIRS 1

This refers primarily to some operational measures and
secret work in conjunction with the Federal Counterin-
telligence Service and the MVD [Ministry of Internal
Affairs]. Russian lawmakers have given their customs
officers this right. Moreover, customs officers can now
also close a border. For instance, there is no state border
between Russia and Kazakhstan—and, consequently, no
border guards either.

So Turkmenistan is saying—our lawmakers have not
given us these powers, and so we cannot accept this.

In short, the situation that has developed in the course of
the work on the Basic Principles of Customs Legislation
is a difficult one and is compounded once again by the
position adopted by Ukraine, which, in the opinion of
analysts, is for quite incomprehensible reasons failing to
show any interest in maintaining direct contacts with
producers, entrepreneurs, and businessmen in the Com-
monwealth countries. The Russian State Customs Com-
mittee is acutely aware of the difference between
Ukraine’s declared readiness for integration and its
practical actions. Moreover, the change of leadership in
that country—including in the customs department—
has led to a hardline attitude as regards the creation of a
customs union (something that had not previously been
in evidence).

Nonetheless, the work to form the union has been
moving ahead quite dynamically. The last Council of
CIS Heads of Government meeting adopted an inte-
grated methodology for compiling customs statistics.
Currently not a single country of the former union has a
mechanism for compiling foreign economic statistics.
The Russian Committee for Statistics [Roskomstat, as
published] uses figures supplied by the State Customs
Committee. It alone records cargo on the border and
enters that information into its data base. A communi-
cations network for combating drugs smuggling has been
set up, and Moscow is compiling a data base on drugs
shipments. Work is under way to unify tariff and non-
tariff relations with third countries. These should be
unified, otherwise it will be impossible to avoid reex-
ports. In short, the customs officers are hopeful that the
union will start operating in early 1996.

2 INTERREGIONAL AFFAIRS

ROSSHSKAYA GAZETA carries an interview with
Alexander Lebed, Commander of the 14th Russian
Army stationed in the Dniester region.

Asked whether he is prepared to join in political struggle,
Lebed said: “I do not take any political steps. I express
my attitude as a citizen of Russia who does not intend to
go anywhere out of the country...”

In response to a question whether he sees politicians in
Russia and Moldova who could really change the situa-
tion for the better, Lebed said: “I see such politicians but
I would not like to give the names for ethical consider-
ations. Whoever is named, with our level of democracy,
a person, against his own will and my will, may become
the focus of close attention and scrutiny. I would not like
to let anyone down.”

granted a 6,000-million-dollar loan for the stabilisation
of the rouble, this would have been followed by an
increase in the share of credits for Russia among other
lenders from 10 percent in 1994 to 30 percent in 1995.

In the case of Chechnya, this Federation Council power
was not required—the president has instituted neither a

without relying on the clearly expressed will of the entire

“prewar” stage. The house’s inactivity in this situation
looks strange not because it failed to carry out its
constitutional obligations (I reiterate that it did not come
to that), but because the Federation Council, while
representing the interests of Federation components,
had adopted for consideration many issues without any
direct relevance to its powers (the defaulting crisis,
economic and social policy, the fight against crime, and
so forth), but had not taken any action and had not put
forward for debate the question of the situation in the
Chechen Republic—which, under the Constitution, is
just as much a Federation component as all the rest. In
this event it is hardly justifiable that the Chechen
Republic is not represented by deputies in the Federa-

prompted the Federation Council leadership to have
initiated “diplomatic” and conciliation procedures to

decrees reflecting the will of all Federation components
at the most) to bring the full force of the house’s prestige
to bear on both the Chechen leaders and the federal
executive and president. The will of Federation Council
deputies and the chamber’s attitude toward the situation
in Chechnya was expressed only after the military-police

unilateral guarantees of similar content by the PRC and
France.

At the same time, given the enormous significance of
these documents for our national security, one has to
realize that Ukraine cannot remain aloof from the gen-
eral Furopean debates on a new architecture of security
that will characterize the European landscape in the 21Ist

Realistically assessing Ukraine’s geopolitical situation

That is why the principle of integral security has become

security in a divided Europe; nor can such a system be
effective unless each of its members has equal rights and

Ukraine, which once proclaimed its intention to become

sphere. Being geographically a Central-Eastern European
country, where Europe’s geographical center is situated,
Ukraine seems to be destined by nature to play the role
of a link between the political East and West.

One of the possible mechanisms for achieving the goal of
building a new trans-Atlantic security system, involving
all countries of “an enlarged Europe,” is the develop-
ment and strengthening of existing security institutions
in the broad meaning of the word (CSCE, NATO,
Western European Union, European Union) and their
interaction and mutual openness to negotiations rather
than competition. In other words, today the main ques-
tion of a future architecture of European security is a
kind of “division of duties” among the existing struc-
tures for achieving a common goal.

That is why the question of where Ukraine must be—in
the East or in the West—is academic from the point of
view of strategic and general European dimensions.
Ukraine is situated at the center of Europe. The problem
of “expanding NATO” must also be viewed in this
respect. This process seems to be an objective one, since

itself to consider, in principle, conditions for its numer-
ical expansion. Here, there cannot be any logical objec-

Arms Stockpile in Kaliningrad After Russian
Withdrawal

LD2812135194 Vilnius Radio Vilnius Network in
Lithuanian 1000 GMT 28 Dec 94

{[FBIS Translated Text] After the withdrawal of the
Russian Baltic Navy from Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia,
and Poland, 35,000 tons of extra scale ammunition of
various purpose, equivalent to 10,000 tons of TNT
explosives, have accumulated in Kaliningrad. At present
70 percent of the Russian Baltic Navy ammunition,
which until the withdrawal of the Russian military forces
was in the Baltic states is now found in Kaliningrad.

A plan for the reduction of Russian Baltic Navy arma-
ments has been set up until the year 2000. Almost 800
railway carriages of ammunition—out of the 3,000 cur-
rently present in the region—have been taken out for
destruction this year in parts of central Russia and the
Leningrad region.

Explosives of rated power are destroyed locally in the
Kaliningrad region, however, there are no grounds avail-
able in the Kaliningrad region for destruction of aviation
bombs. The Baltic Sea is too shallow to bury the explo-
sives.

The Baltic Sea Navy pins great hopes on the sea ferry
Kaliningrad-St. Petersburg, which is to be opened in the
future. It is believed that this would make the transpor-
tation of the ammunition that had accumulated out of
the region easier. At present the explosives are being
taken out of the Kaliningrad region by support vessels,
however, this way of transportation is inefficient and
expensive.

As an alternative it has been proposed to set up a
utilization center for rated power ammunition locally at
the Baltic Sea Navy bases. There is such a proposal,
however, the Russian Ministry of Defense has not given
its approval yet. So far, the Baltic Sea Navy is reducing
the pile of ammunition accumulated in the region with
their own resources. Next year it is expected to reduce
the number of ammunition storage sites by 40 percent
and in future to give up this kind of ammunition storage
altogether.

Two Charged With Illegal Possession of Uranium

LD2812212394 Vilnius Radio Vilnius Network in
Russian 1300 GMT 28 Dec 94

{[FBIS Translated Text] ELTA reports that on Tuesday
[27 December], Juozas Vilcinskas and Valerijonas Gar-
bauskas, both residents of Kaunas and Kaunas rayon,
were officially charged by officials from the Lithuanian
General Procuracy Department of Investigation of Orga-
nized Crime and Corruption with illegal possession of
eight kilograms of radioactive uranium.

As a result of investigation by the State Security Depart-
ment and the General Procuracy, eight kilograms of
radioactive uranium were found in their possession on
15 December.

Over the last weeks, searches have been carried out in
Kaunas, Visaginas, Utena, and Vilnius. The case is
concerned not only with the eight kilograms of radioac-
tive uranium that were found, but with about 100
kilograms more of this material that investigators have
searched for.

Vilcinskas and Garbauskas, who were arrested two
weeks ago, tried to sell the radioactive uranium for
$2,000 a kilogram; later they reduced the price to $1,500
a kilogram.

Analysis of the confiscated uranium is being carried out
to determine where it was used. According to Jurgis
Jurgelis, director general of the State Security Depart-
ment, there are a number of versions concerning the
appearance of the uranium in the country. The two main
versions are: if the specialists confirm that this type of
uranium was used as fuel at nuclear power stations, then
it is possible that the uranium got into the hands of the
arrested from the Ignalina nuclear power station or some
other nuclear power station. Two years ago a cassette
with nuclear fuel went missing at the Ignalina nuclear
power station. The other version is that the uranium got
into the hands of the criminals before it was used as fuel
at any nuclear power station.

There is no doubt that the uranium was stolen because
there is no free trade in nuclear materials anywhere in
the world. Most countries, including Lithuania, have
signed an agreement on nonproliferation of nuclear
materials.
