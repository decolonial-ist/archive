Pamfilova Criticizes Zhirinovskiy Conduct

964F0537A Moscow ARGUMENTY I FAKTY
in Russian Dec 95 No 49, p 3

{Interview with Ella Pamfilova by Galina Valyuzhenich,
Aleksandr Sargin, and Veronika Sivkova, in Moscow;
date not given: "A Dialogue That Did Not Take Place.
The Falcon Did Not Fly In"}

{(FBIS Translated Text] On Monday at 1730, a meet-
ing was supposed to take place at our editorial offices
between the leader of the Pamfilova-Gurov-V. Lysenko
bloc, Ella Pamfilova, and Vladimir Zhirinovskiy. There
were many television and print journalists there. At
1800, an aide of Vladimir Volfovich’s called the edi-
torial offices and, with apologies, informed us that his
boss had been slightly detained. In another half-hour
there was another call, advising us that Zhirinovskiy was
already driving up. And after 30 more minutes, it was
reported that the leader of the LDPR [Liberal- Demo-
cratic Party of Russia} would not appear at the meeting.
It would be interesting to know with what such change-
ability in his promises is connected. If over an hour
and a half he changed his decision three times, one can
imagine how many times he will be able to do this once
he gets into the Duma again. Or maybe he consists only
of promises in the first place? But the questions had
piled up—both the editorial offices and E. Pamfilova
had them. And therefore, a conversation could not fail
to take place....

[ARGUMENTY I FAKTY] Ella Aleksandrovna, what
do you think, why did Zhirinovskiy not come?

{Pamfilova] I don’t know; it is the election campaign
now, and everyone has a lot to do. After all, he is not
afraid of me, is he?! Although....have you noticed the
fear in his eyes? He has so many security guards. I, for
example, do not have any. What do you need this for,
Vladimir Volfovich? Why do you insist that deputies need
to be issued weapons? Do your constant hysterics and
shrieks not stem from a feeling of fear and of uncertainty
in yourself? What scares you so much?

[ARGUMENTY I FAKTY] Yes, really, one does not
just have a lot of security guards for no reason.
Probably, there is something that needs protecting. All
those tours of his around the hinterlands, where he is
met in Mercedes, to the strains of an orchestra....

{Pamfilova] I also do not understand why he drives
around in two Mercedes, and with flashing lights, to
boot. Recently I found myself in Astrakhan at the
same time with him. Zhirinovskiy flew in, in his own
airplane, rented out a theater and a cafe. Where do such
funds come from? Where does the money come from to

hold birthday parties for 1,500 people? By the way, he
invited me as well.

Why do you, Vladimir Volfovich, constantly try to sink
a law on limiting deputy immunity, in which we also
insisted that all declarations of deputies’ income and
property be published? Is this not a consequence of the
fact that you have too much money? Those 11 billion
rubles in your coffers: What is that—payment for what
has already been worked out in the Duma, or an advance
for the future?

[ARGUMENTY I FAKTY] But you also in your
time were engaged in a struggle against privilege and
corruption, and then abandoned it.

{[Pamfilova] No, I never abandoned it, and I do not
plan to. We now have with us in our association A.
Gurov, who first declared that there was a mafia in the
USSR and who created the Organized Crime Directorate
within the Ministry of Internal Affairs. In 1992, he was
dismissed as being inconvenient.

Now those at the top do not want to deal seriously with
these issues. Therefore, the fight against corruption is
taking the form of information on personal accounts.
There is compromising material on many people. But it
is used not to bring a criminal case, but so as to be able
to manipulate them more easily.

The law ought to be the same for all. And the higher
you are, the higher your responsibility to society, to
the authorities, before the law. And that same high
demand should begin from the very "top." And with
oneself. Is that not true, Vladimir Volfovich? You are
always announcing that you are in profound opposition,
that the authorities are such-and-such.... But allow me
to disagree with you. I believe that your faction is
very convenient and one of the most loyal to the
current authorities, almost a tame faction. If one looks
realistically at the results of your voting, that is the way
it is. You supported the unleashing of war in Chechnya;
you were wholly on the side of the authorities. You
always supported your friend Grachev. You supported
the antisocial budget. So that means you support this
government.

[ARGUMENTY I FAKTY] But some people blame
precisely you for the fact that a pension crisis has burst
forth.

{Pamfilova] I know about that. But I have nothing to
make excuses for. While I was minister, we did not
have this kind of outrage. I warned the president a year
and a half ago of a pension crisis. It could have been
averted. And we proposed a multitude of variants. How
many trillions are now in Chechnya? Experts say that
it has come to more that 30.... They find the money for

KRO Initiative Impact Analyzed

964F0539A Moscow ARGUMENTY I FAKTY
in Russian Dec 95 No 49, p7 ;

[Article by Aleksandr Simonov, under the rubric
"Elections-95": "Russian Politics Is in Turmoil"]

(FBIS Translated Text] Russian political circles are ex-
citedly discussing the unexpected initiative the Congress
of Russian Communities [KRO] suddenly brought out at
the eleventh hour of the election campaign. As far back
as the KRO’s program congress in mid- November its
delegates decided to initiate a national referendum on
the “main issues of Russia’s future.” It was thought at
the time, however, that the rather cumbersome proce-
dure envisaged by the law on referendum would stop
Skokov, busy storming the State Duma. Nonetheless,

he did register in the Moscow city electoral commis-
sion, and so the process got started.

The proposal is to make “increasing the well-being
and longevity of the population" the main criterion
of the government’s work. In addition, it is proposed
to constitutionally define the Russian Federation as a
"Union of Peoples of Russia."

KRO leader Yuriy Skokov said that he considers such
a referendum even more important for himself than
success in the State Duma elections, since Russian
Citizens’ positive reply to the proffered questions will
let the KRO create a real mechanism of public control
over the actions of state authorities.

It is easy to see that questions are selected in such a way
as to directly affect each citizen of Russia. Therefore,
it is quite natural that the KRO’s unexpected initiative,
on top of that announced at the peak of the election
campaign, got quite a response (even KOMMERSANT,
normally quite critical of the KRO, called the question
of linking appraisal of the government’s performance
with the population’s standard of living the “question
of the week").

The Public Opinion foundation conducted on 18
November a sociological survey of a national sample
of 1,360 persons to sound out people’s attitude to the
referendum proposed by the KRO. According to this
survey’s data, 84 percent of the Russian population
believe it necessary to put the Russian authorities under
greater control by the people. Seventy percent of the
respondents would participate in a referendum on the
question of amending the RF [Russian Federation]
Constitution to envisage the possibility of forcing the
government to resign if it does not succeed in stopping
the decline in the Russian population’s standard of
living. Also, 67 percent would vote for incorporating
such an amendment in the text of the Constitution.
And 83 percent believe that the subsistence minimum
should be legislatively formalized.

Seventy-five percent would take part in a referendum
on the question of toughening the measures to combat
crime; with this, 74 percent support the broader use of
Capital punishment, and 17 percent are against capital
punishment.

The reaction of Gennadiy Zyuganov, head of the CPRF
[Communist Party of the Russian Federation], whose
leadership considers the KRO its main rival in the up-
coming parliamentary elections, was quite cautious. He
said that in order to define the CPRF’s final position
on the referendum "we must first thoroughly review
and discuss the issue." He also remarked that "patri-
otic forces have already put forward similar initiatives

in the past.” Interestingly, at exactly the same time an-

porting the latest rumors about serious disagreements in
the top echelons of the CPRF.

St. Petersburg Mayor Anatoliy Sobchak—one of active
supporters of Chernomyrdin’s Russia Is Our Home
[NDR] bloc—said, naturally, that he is firmly against
holding a referendum. Sobchak fears that a provision
that will make the government's stay in power strictly
dependent on the population’s standard of living can
be used “as a political truncheon to clobber a political
opponent.” For Russia, Sobchak believes, “the important
thing is not to make any sharp moves," otherwise "we
will again enter a period of instability.”

Another representative of the NDR government bloc,
Vladimir Ryzhkov, while acknowledging that defining
the question of the population’s standard of living as
a criterion of the government’s performance is correct,
nevertheless pointed out that organizing a referendum
would be “disastrously expensive for the country.”

As to the opinion of liberal political forces, Vladimir
Braginskiy, press secretary to YABloko leader Yavlin-
skiy, said that the movement has no interest in the idea
of holding a referendum. He sarcastically remarked that
the meaning of the questions proposed by the KRO
tempts one to add one more question, namely: "Do you
believe that the first letter in the alphabet is A and the
last one is Z?"

Democratic Cheice of Russia, PRES [Party of Russian
Unity and Accord], and Women of Russia under various
excuses refused to comment on the KRO initiative.
Apparently, the referendum idea caught these parties’
leaderships by surprise. Only Common Cause leader
Irina Khakamada was very unequivocal. She called
the KRO initiative “irresponsible and unacceptable.”
Holding such a referendum, in Khakamada’s opinion,
“can undermine the current political system, which in
turn is fraught with the danger of civil war.”

LDPR [Liberal Democratic Party of Russia] represen-
tative Viktor Filatov took a somewhat unexpected po-
sition. Despite the fact that relations between this party
and the KRO leave something to be desired, to put it
mildly, the press secretary overall appraised the idea of
holding a referendum positively. He did, though, sharply
criticize General Aleksandr Lebed, saying that the lat-
ter personifies “everything that is worst in the military.”

Filatov also found it necessary to add that "many par-
ties today have beautiful programs that could be framed
and hung on the wall, but they are all the same.” The
LDPR secretary apparently believes that ciiy his party
has a program that is “not the same.” Neither can one
leave out the opinion of the veteran of Russian poli-
tics, Arkadiy Volskiy. He believes that the referendum
proposed by the KRO is not needed. In his opinion,
the result of an affirmative reply at the referendum will
be that not a singie government will be able to stay in
power in Russia because “bread prices will keep rising
no matter what.”

Youth electoral bloc “The Cusp Generation" in its
Statement fully supported “Skokov's initiative" (while
avoiding proclaiming solidarity with the KRO). The
bloc’s leaders justify their position citing the fact that
the needed amendments to the Constitution can be made
only through a referendum.

It appears that the leader of another sociopolitical
movement— Sergey Redko of People’s Policy, who is
also an MZhK [expansion unidentified] Iskra activist—
is in agreement with this opinion. He believes that “the
government today is not accountable to anyone, and
therefore bureaucrats leading the state toward a disaster
are confident of their immunity.” Sergey Redko believes
that through a referendum, amendments could be made
to the Constitution that would give the Duma the ability
to form the government. Redko also pointed out that he
supports the healthy idea, not the KRO. "This is not a
vote of confidence in Skokov,” says Redko. "We support
a concrete initiative. The current Constitution guarantees
us rights that are still not realized because the existing
bodies of authority are not accountable.” Especially, a
Redko’s opinion, local bodies of authority.

RF State Duma Deputy Vyacheslav Nikonov said the
following on the subject of a referendum: “I have to
say that the Constitution definitely ought to be amended.
The KRO’s proposals are somewhat demagogic, how-
ever, since there is a host of methodologies for eval-
uating the population’s standard of living. But even if
such a decision (to hold a referendum—aA.S.) is made,
this will create a new precedent in international practice,
since in civilized countries the government is account-
able to the parliament and its existence depends on the
will of the majority in the parliament. So if the situation
in the country worsens, the government resigns at the
parliament's decision, not at the whim of statistics. '

As we can see, Russian politicians’ reaction to the
KRO’s initiative turned out to be varied but overall
rather guarded. Representatives of some movements
altogether declined to answer the question of their
attitude to the referenduta idea, while a clear difference

{Article by Andrey Uglanov under the rubric "Money":

Duma elections. Parties and movements are throwing
money right and left. Television, newspapers, advertis-

nomic Freedom Party, B. Polevanov’s For the Mother-
land!, Sv. Fedorov’s Workers’ Self-Government Party,
V. Tyulkin’s and V. Anpilov’s Communists, Stanislav
Govorukhin’s Bloc, N. Ryzhkov’s Power to the People!,
and G. Popov’s Social-Democrats. These parties, while

964F0538A Moscow ARGUMENTY I FAKTY
in Russian Dec 95 No 49, p 3

{Interview with Vlevolod Bogdanov, chairman of the
Journalists Union of Russia, by ARGUMENTY I
FAKTY correspondent V. Viktorov; place and date not
given; under the rubric "Campaign Tour": "Why Does

(FBIS Translated Text] Vsevolod Bogdanov has headed
the Journalists Union of Russia for more than three
years. During these extremely trying times for the press,
the union became a prominent force in society.

Previously sovereign journalist associations of Moscow,
St. Petersburg, Tatarstan, and Bashkortostan joined

In the event the candidate proposed for the post of
chairman of the Bank of Russia is rejected, the president

more than twice.

One and the same person may not hold the post of

terms in a row.

the Bank of Russia from the post at the submission of
the president of the Russian Federation.

The chairman of the Bank of Russia may be dis-
missed from the post only in the following cases:

1) the term of office expires;

2) the inability to perform official duties confirmed
by the report of a state medical commission;

force;

[Article by Viktor Kuznetsov: “Russian Oil and Foreign
Investment; Foreign Companies Are Leaving Russia,

[FBIS Translated Text] In its history, spanning more
than a century, the domestic oil industry has produced
almost 14 billion tonnes of oil, and around 40 percent
of the total was produced in the last 10-12 years.

The output in Russia at the beginning of the 20th century
was around 8.8 million tonnes a year. The output of oil
in 1940 was 3.4 times as great as in 1913. In 1946 Stalin
insisted on an annual output of 60 million tonnes, and
this was the aim of the three postwar five-year plans:
"This is the only way we can protect ourselves against
any eventuality." By 1955 the output already exceeded
70 million tonnes, and by the beginning of the 1960s it
had reached 147 million tonnes a year.

In the 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s, a period distinguished
by the discovery and incorporation of the large oil fields
of the Ural-Volga zone and West Siberia, the annual in-
crease in oil production exceeded 20-25 million tonnes.
This was a result of an emphasis on the exploitation of
highly productive oil pools. The oil from active, eas-
ily accessible deposits was extracted at a rate of 10-12
percent a year. The depletion of strata in the oil fields
of Tyumen Oblast now exceeds 30 percent, and the fig-

ures for other regions are 70 percent for the Volga-
Vyatka zone and 80 percent for the Northern Caucasus.
Oil wells with a low yield, producing under 25 tonnes of
oil a day, now represent 85 percent of the total, as com-
pared to 37 percent in 1981; the proportional amount of
highly productive wells, yielding more than 50 tonnes
a day, dropped from 51 percent to 5 percent during the
same period. The oil from most wells has a high rate
of water contamination—up to 85-90 percent. There are
still sizable quantities of retained oil, however, in the
blind zones and dead zones of oil pools.

Known reserves of the black gold in Russia amount to
25 billion tonnes, a figure surpassed only by the known
reserves in Saudi Arabia. Around 900 oil deposits
are being worked in our country—all the way from
Kaliningrad to Sakhalin, and from Kolguyev Island in
the north to the foothills of the Caucasus in the south.
West Siberia, with 66 percent of the output of black gold
and 70 percent of all known reserves, is the country’s
main oil-producing region. Most of the unexplored
resources are also concentrated there, although they also
exist in East Siberia, the European north, and the shelf
of the Arctic and Far Eastern seas.

Oil production in Russia reached its highest point—
569.5 million tonnes—in 1988. By 1995 it had dropped
to 317 million tonnes and is still falling.

Only 185.7 million tonnes of oil reached Russian
refineries in 1994. The rest was exported. Even in earlier
years—the perestroyka and pre-perestroyka years—only
half of our oil was refined at home. The rest was sold
to foreign states.

Crude oil prices began to rise dramatically in 1974,
reaching 234-249 dollars a tonne by the beginning of
the 1980s, as compared to 18 dollars in fall 1973. It
is true that these prices dropped to 95-103 dollars a
tonne soon afterward, but in December 1986 the OPEC
countries agreed to raise the price of a tonne of exported
oil to 132 dollars. Today it is even higher. It rose 21
dollars in the second quarter of 1995. Since 15 March
1995 Russian oil has been sent to the CIS countries at
125 dollars a tonne and to the Baltic countries at world
prices.

No other country in the world has even experienced the
kind of dramatic growth of the oil and gas industry that
occurred in the USSR in the middle of the 1970s. The
development of new areas in West Siberia quickly led
to a 3.3-fold increase in the oil output and a 6.4-fold
increase in gas production. The days when the high
rates of oil production were secured by the intensive
exploitation of active reserves in conveniently located
fields, however, are gone and will never return. Most of
Russia’s oil today is concentrated in almost inaccessible

the constant lag between the rate of rise in the dollar

sooner or later to higher client demand for foreign

[FBIS Translated Text] Russia’s banking system is five
years old this year. The foundation for its formation
was laid by an order of the chairman of the board
of the RSFSR State Bank of 17 August 1990, in
accordance with which USSR Gosbank administrations
were turned into RSFSR State Bank administrations.
Many problems were surmounted during the past years.
Mutual nonpayments, the “gold fever" of easy money,
“black Tuesday” and many other problems have been
left behind. In spite of everything, Russia’s banking
system has not only remained standing, but has grown
stronger. It is as hard to compare its present-day power
— by the end of September there were over 2,500
commercial banks re, in Russia — with the
situation characteristic for 1991 as it is to discern the
breadth and scale of the Volga in the Samara area on
the basis of a little river in the Urals.

The evolution of commercial banks in the past five years
may theoretically be broken up into three stagcs. The
first is the usual commercial activity, the basic goal of
which was to get onto their feet and gather strength.
After that, “bristling” with funds and communications,
the bank passes to the second stage — it becomes
a social institution, necessary to society, performing
the very important function of handling the monetary
transactions of economic agents. The bank does not so
far, however, take part in the political life of the country.
Most Russian banks, of those which are five years old,
have already passed the first stage of formation and are
in the second stage. The largest banks have passed 'o
the third stage of development and possess noticeable
political weight.

But why then is it precisely today that there is so much
talk about the instability of the banking system, so much
uneasiness about its future? The question of a coming

wave of bankruptcy has already arisen more than once
recently. Where does the truth lie — in the strength that
makes it possible to survive and grow stronger in the
economy, or in the weakness which overcomes many

There is obviously no inconsistency here. It happens this
way in life — when a person passes from adolescence
to maturity, he surprisingly combines within himself
both strength and weakness at the same time. Something
like that is now happening to the banking system. The
banks, just as Russian society, have not solved many
of their problems, and their overall situation therefore
remains unstable. At the same time, this does not mean
that the role of the banks as economic institutions is
fundamentally a humble one. The role of the banks
should be evaluated, not according to a particular result,
which we can see at present, but according to their
overall purpose — to serve the interests of realizing
society’s need for credit institutions. In practice, this
means that each bank should assist in protecting the
interests of all its clients. We know that the specific
products of a bank are deposit, credit and clearing
transactions. This is where the positive purpose of banks
as enterprises lies. But this is also where the banks are,
for the time being, proving to be weak.

The main reason for the weakness of the banking sector
is the crisis situation in the economy and the attendant
crisis in society’s faith in credit institutions. The people,
who have for decades experienced for themselves the
permissiveness of the authorities, and are exhausted
by the endless race of inflation, often do not trust
commercial banks, not because they are not a reliable
place in which to keep monetary funds, but because our
recent history has taught them this. Let us remember
the way the people first stood in line to hand over their
money to savings banks because of rumors about the
coming monetary reform, and then rushed to remove
their deposits for fear of their being frozen.

Unfortunately, the RF Central Bank has recently been
following a policy which places the banks in a serious
economic position. I mean the compulsory bank reserve.
The aim of this policy is understandable in principle —
to take rubles out of circulation, and through immobi-
lization of the ruble, “to beat down" the exchange rate of
the dollar, in order to ensure the conditions for attract-
ing the loans of international organizations and simulta-
neously create reserves to extend credit for the budget
deficit. In this situation, many banks have two ways out
left to them: either cease to exist or go begging to the
Bank of Russia. Today — this is a reality. If a bank is
not solvent, it is forced to agree fully with the position
of the Bank of Russia, and then there is a chance that
they will help and support the bank. If the bank does not

agree, it will be appointed a temporary administration,
because of its pre-bankruptcy situation, and the bank’s
management will be relieved of its work.

It is not only the policy followed by the Central Bank
and the instability of the macro-economic situation
that has caused the crisis state of Russian banks,
however. The banks’ own tactical errors also led to
it. Unfortunately, quite a large part of the banks in
Russia have shareholders — major sectorial structures
— who thrust on the banks a policy of protectionist
credit. For the sake of satisfying the needs of their
leading shareholders, many banks extend credit to
inefficient projects, thereby risking the profit of the
client, and sometimes his capital as well. This type of
credit extension is becoming the scourge of the Russian
banking system, and it threatens to undermine the bases
of the credit policy and reinforce the trend toward the
bankruptcy of credit institutions.

There is another category of banks for which, as before,
the main task is the pursuit of the “easy” and “quick”
tuble. Analysis shows that most of the banks at one
time tempted clients with super-high interest and issued
loans to unreliable clients. Unjustified risks turned
into nonpayments and a serious financial situation.
Especially important, in periods of exacerbated financial
difficulties, these banks as a rule sharply stepped up their
activity as a borrower on the interbank credit market.
This led to the nonliquidity of them and of their clients,
and of the whole chain of partners, creating a "domino"
effect.

Because of this, making the banking system healthy and
closing banks which are undermining the foundations of
the financial sphere are becoming not only inevitable,

be classified in the same way. It appears that the idea of
supporting banks and averting their bankruptcy merits
attention only when the violation of the principles of
conducting banking business proves to be quite serious
and might entail the loss of the monetary funds of the
clients (enterprises and the people). It would therefore
be useful:

In the first place, to create in the country a mechanism
to avert the bankruptcies of credit institutions, which
would lead to a loss of the clients’ resources;

In the second place, to determine, by norm, the mini-
mum total amount of credit for a bank’s shareholders,
not to exceed the vulume of the resources drawn in;

In the third place, in banks subject to reorganization, to

Banks deal with money — the sharpest weapon of

eatire sectors. In themselves, banks are not only an

not because these instruments are obsolete,

instrument which accelerates social development has
always been credit — a mover of economic

not by means of administrative measures, but in a

The banks are already a powerful economic force and

[Shatalov] Many countries, like Russia today, are get-

Baku, Moscow Caspian Stances Contrasted

964F0S62A Yerevan RESPUBLIKA ARMENIYA
in Russian 8 Dec 95 pl

[Article by Karen Topchyan: “Azerbaijan: The Consti-
tution and the Status of the Caspian: Is a Compromise
With Moscow Possible?"]

(FBIS Translated Text) Azerbaijan is continuing the
diplomatic confrontation with Moscow, which, Baku
officialdom believes, should signify its even further
estrangement from its northern neighbor. It is a question,
specifically, of the constitution of this country, which
was adopted recently, g that the Azerbaijani
sector of the Caspian shall henceforward be considered
this country’s territory. Now, as Baku analysts believe,
following the adoption of the country’s new basic law,
nonrecognition for Azerbaijan of the right to its sector in
the Caspian on the part of Moscow couid also hereby be
interpreted as an expression of doubt as to the legitimacy
of the country’s constitution.

But such problems are of no great concern to Moscow
itself, it would seem. The Russian Ministry of Foreign
Affairs sent Baku a protest note, which expresses dis-
agreement with the ee of the Con-
stitution of Azerbaijan. And Mikhail Demurin, official
spokesmen of Russia’s foreign policy department, de-

FBIS-SOV-95-241-S
15 December 1995

clared plainly in an interview with BBC Radio: "We
pr from the fact that, according to international
aw, the Caspian is neither as a whole nor in any com-
ponent a part of the territory of any Caspian state.” Many
people in Azerbaijan believe that such forceful expres-
sions of Russian diplomats could have a very inauspi-
cious effect on the competitiveness of the Russian oil
muy LUKoil. I would recall that LUKoil’s share in
the first international consortium for the development of
the oil fields on the territory of Azerbaijan constitutes 10
percent, and in the second, 32.5 percent. Spokesmen for
LUKoil itself in Baku had a very indifferent response
to Moscow’s demarche. "I cannot be responsible for the
Russian Ministry of Foreign Affairs spokesman on this
matter," Vladimir Ivanov, leader of the company’s Baku
office, declared.

As far as the Azerbaijani leadership is concerned, then,
according to Foreign Minister Gasan Gasanov, “the
difference between the positions of Azerbaijan and
Russia is that we are for determination of the status
of the Caspian on the basis of international practice,
while Russia believes that it should be determined on
the basis simply of the desire of one individual country."
“Our position," the Azerbaijani minister continues, “is
that all lakes that are littoral are divided by the sectorial
principle. The lakes between the United States and
Canada and France, Switzerland, and Germany and the
lakes in Central Africa are such."

ae about “the desire of an individual country,"
anov was undoubtedly referring to Russia. But
Moscow also, in turn, appeals to quite telling arguments
to substantiate its position concerning the status of
the Caspian. According to the Soviet-Iranian treaties
of 1921 and 1940 (the sole acts in international law
determining the status of this inland sea, incidentally),
this is a lake, which cannot be divided into national
sectors. Today the position of the Caspian countries
is such: Russia, Iran, and Turkmenistan cleave to the
"lake" viewpoint, that is, advocate joint use of the
Caspian. Azerbaijan and, to some extent, Kazakhstan
are for it being considered a sea, that is, its division into
national sectors. So it is still too early to speak about
total clarity as regards the production of Azerbaijani
oil and its transportation to West Europe even more.
LUKoil’s participation in the oil projects by no means
testifies to Russia's recognition for Azerbaijan of the
right to "dominion" off its shores. Moscow is proposing
either the creation of a joint body of the five Caspian
states, which would issue licenses for oil production, or
a joint Caspian company. Whatever the case, according
to Albert Chernyshev, deputy minister of foreign affairs
of Russia, “there is as yet no final version, and the
way toward it will lie, in any event, via a series
of compromises among the Caspian states." But the
whole question is how far Baku and Moscow are really
disposed toward mutual concessions.
